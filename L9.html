Plain-looking rocks, very large deposits

Lecture 6 showed metal-rich fluid flowing out onto today's seafloor. SEDEX deposits are related to these vents, but they are cooler, saltier and hosted in sediment. Salty basin brines leak up faults into a still sea that contains sulphide. They deposit zinc and lead as very thin layers in black mud. The rock looks plain, but these are the largest zinc and lead deposits in the world, and northern Australia has more of them than any other region. We start with the one property that decides what kind of deposit a brine forms: whether it floats or sinks.

What a SEDEX deposit is

means sedimentary exhalative. Fluid with metals flows out (exhales) into a reduced sedimentary basin inside a continent or on its edge. The metals are deposited as sulphides on the basin floor and just below it. The fluids are salty brines. They are warm, not hot: above about 80 °C and mostly below 200 °C. The ore bodies are flat and tabular, with more zinc than lead ( and ), some silver, and often as gangue. The ore is in layers between iron sulphides (pyrite and pyrrhotite) and the sedimentary rocks of the basin. This is why most geologists think these deposits are : they formed at the same time as the sediment. slide 7

They belong to a group of hydrothermal exhalative deposits. deposits are linked to volcanic rocks (Lecture 10). SEDEX deposits, also called sediment-hosted massive sulphide (SHMS) deposits, are in sedimentary basins. and Irish-type deposits are covered later in the course. slide 6

Show what I see
  • The layers are millimetres thick, very flat, and continuous across the 10 cm slab. There is only gentle wavy disturbance.
  • There are three kinds of band: thin pale layers, thin dark layers, and one thicker, speckled grey band with a sharp base that becomes finer upwards.
  • No veins cut the layers. There is no breccia and no lump of massive sulphide.

So this is sediment that was deposited layer by layer. The thick graded band is a . The dark layers are black shale, and the thin layers between them are sphalerite and galena. The sulphides were deposited together with the mud, layer by layer. This is the main observation behind the syngenetic model.

This is the surprise of SEDEX: the name "massive sulphide" is not really correct. Most of the ore is fine sulphide in thin layers (laminae) between shale and siltstone layers. It is not a solid lump of sulphide. A piece of Century ore is just a black carbonaceous siltstone with a little sphalerite and galena along the layers and fractures. It looks plain, but these deposits are world class. lecture recording, 2 Oct

In fact, the McArthur River mine has been operating since the mid 1990s, when ultra-fine grinding first made it possible to process the ore. Memorise
  • SEDEX: venting of saline brine (about 80 to 200 °C) into a reduced intracratonic or epicratonic sedimentary basin.
  • Tabular, Zn > Pb (sphalerite, galena) plus Ag, barite gangue, interbedded with pyrite, pyrrhotite and basin sediment.
  • Fine laminated ore, mostly syngenetic; HYC is the classic example.

Context 13 to 26 m

Why zinc and lead matter

Zinc has a long history. The Charaka Samhita, an Indian text from about 500 BC, mentions zinc that oxidised to "philosopher's wool" (zinc oxide). It was used as an ointment for the eyes and for open wounds. Zinc smelting moved from India to China in the 17th century, and the first European zinc smelter opened in Bristol in 1743. Zinc oxide is still used in calamine cream, antiseptic ointments and sunscreen. slide 2

Today, most zinc is used for steel (about 60%). The rest is used for die-cast alloys (about 13%), brass (about 11%), and oxides and chemicals (about 8%). Electric vehicle makers prefer galvanised steel for car bodies. Zinc coatings stop the frames of solar panels and wind turbines from rusting. Zinc-ion batteries are considered safer than lithium-ion batteries. Zinc can be 100% recycled without losing quality, but only about 30% of the zinc produced comes from recycled sources. slides 2 and 3

Concept 1 26 to 39 m

What a basin needs

Five things must occur together: an extensional setting; structures that control the shape of the basin and the fluid flow; a thick basin fill; large-scale hydrothermal fluid flow; and still (stagnant) water that contains H₂S. slide 10

The basin is a continental rift with 2 to 5 km of permeable clastic sediments, often with volcanic or volcaniclastic rocks. These coarse sediments are the that holds the brine. Above them are impermeable shales or carbonates deposited after the rifting. These are the . Without this cover, the fluid leaks away up the faults. Then you get slightly higher metal values everywhere, but no ore deposit. slide 11 lecture recording, 2 Oct

Something must heat the basin. SEDEX deposits form close in time and place to basaltic volcanic rocks, dykes and sills. These increase the geothermal gradient and drive convection. Mafic sills help, but they are not essential: any heat source that keeps the brine convecting will work. Fluid flows out at only a few vent sites, where extensional faults meet transform faults. The basin must also last a long time: 100 to 200 million years. slides 11 and 12 lecture recording, 2 Oct

The sea above must be . This means it is an enclosed basin, with no fresh seawater with oxygen flowing in. Evaporation continues, so a restricted basin also becomes very salty. The deep water fills with H₂S, and this H₂S supplies the sulphur that traps the metals. These are not black-smoker deposits. A SEDEX basin has one or two vent sites that last a long time, not fields of chimneys. lecture recording, 2 Oct

Case study 39 to 52 m

Building the McArthur Basin

The SEDEX basins of northern Australia formed in the Proterozoic, in a setting far from the arc. Subduction was to the south, under what is now the Arunta region, with an arc above it. About 500 km behind the arc, hot asthenosphere rose. It heated and thinned the crust, opened rift and sag basins, and supplied the heat that kept the brines convecting. The McArthur Basin and the Mount Isa Inlier both formed in this way, and both have many SEDEX deposits. slide 13 lecture recording, 2 Oct

The faults are deep and were active for a long time. HYC is next to the Emu Fault Zone. The nearby Hot Springs Fault got its name from the hot springs that are still on it. The Calvert and Tawallah faults form the edges of the Batten Trough. are important because they open (dilate) as they move, so fluid can pass through. Where two faults cross, they open even more. Mechanism 52 to 65 m

Filling and breaking the aquifer

Deep in the coarse syn-rift sediments, compaction and metamorphic reactions produce hot, salty brine with metals. The impermeable cap holds the brine in. So the pressure rises until the aquifer is (geopressured) and breaks the cap rock. The brine is then focused up active faults. Focusing is essential for every ore deposit. If the flow is not focused, it only raises background metal levels. slides 17 and 45 lecture recording, 2 Oct

Brine does not flow steadily. Each small earthquake releases stress on the fault and opens it. A pulse of fluid passes through, and then minerals are deposited and seal the fault again. Each pulse adds another layer of sulphide. The basin goes through different anoxic cycles, so the ore lenses in one basin can have different ages. slides 17 and 26 lecture recording, 2 Oct

Concept 2 65 to 78 m

Vent-proximal or vent-distal: the brine decides

The structure of a SEDEX deposit depends on how close to the vent the sulphides form. This depends on the density of the brine. slide 18

deposits form from buoyant fluids that rise from the vent. They have alteration in the footwall, breccia pipes where overpressured fluid broke the rock, and coarser sulphide close to the vent. deposits form from brine that is denser than seawater. The brine flows away from the vent and collects in low areas on the basin floor. The sulphides are in layers with the sediment and are well laminated. There is no footwall alteration, no breccia zones, and there is related barite. One basin can have both types. slide 18 lecture recording, 2 Oct

Salt makes brine heavier, and heat makes it lighter. A vent-distal brine is more salty, cooler and oxidised. It is a dense brine that stays on the floor, as at HYC. A vent-proximal brine is less salty, hotter and reduced. It is a medium-density brine that rises a little but is not strongly buoyant, as at Sullivan in Canada.   • Vent-distal: dense brine pooled in depressions, laminated sulphide, no footwall alteration, barite; higher salinity, lower T, oxidised (HYC).
  • Fluids: 10 to 30 wt% TDS, 100 to 200 °C (maximum about 250 °C).

Concept 3 78 to 91 m

Fluids, metals and sulphur

Where does the brine come from? From seawater that evaporated at the surface. When seawater evaporates in shallow, restricted parts of the basin, the salty brine that is left sinks and moves down through the sediments. Water that dissolves evaporite minerals (mainly halite) below the surface adds more salt. slide 21

There are two types of brine. In basins with mostly shales and turbidites, like the Selwyn Basin and Sullivan in Canada, the brines are reduced (more H₂S than SO₄²⁻) and acidic. In basins with mostly sandstones and carbonates, like most of northern Australia, they are oxidised (more SO₄²⁻ than H₂S) and almost neutral. slide 22 (Cooke et al., 2000)

Oxidised brines with little sulphur carry much more metal. This is also why barium can travel with them. Barite is barium sulphate, and it is deposited as soon as the fluid meets sulphate. So barium can only move in a chloride brine with little sulphur. The fluids stay below about 300 °C, and this is why most of these deposits contain little copper. slide 3, notes

So where does everything come from? The metals come from the basin fill itself. Brine convecting through the aquifer, driven by the heat source, dissolves them. The sulphur comes from the seawater, as H₂S in the anoxic bottom water. When the two meet, the metal chloride reacts with H₂S and sphalerite is deposited:

ZnCl₂ + H₂S → ZnS + 2H⁺ + 2Cl⁻

Notice that the chloride goes back into the water. So depositing sulphide makes the bottom water even saltier. slides 24 and 25 lecture recording, 2 Oct The isotopes agree with this. Sulphur isotopes have a wide range, δ³⁴S from about -10 to +30‰, because the sulphur came from seawater sulphate that was reduced in different ways. Lead isotopes are uniform, with high μ values. This shows that the lead came from upper-crustal rocks inside the basin. slide 47

Mechanism 91 to 104 m

Making the layers: a layered sea

The water in a SEDEX basin has layers. At the top there is an oxic layer, where Fe³⁺ is insoluble. Below it is a mixing layer, where H₂S meets dissolved Fe²⁺ and pyrite is deposited. At the bottom is the anoxic layer, with more H₂S than sulphate. Brine that enters this bottom layer meets H₂S and deposits sphalerite and galena. Pyrite and silt fall down from above. Clumps of sphalerite grow and sink to the floor by gravity. slide 28 (Ireland et al., 2004)

Three things build the layered ore: organic-rich mud that settles slowly from the water (pelagic), carbonate-rich silt that slides in from the shelf as mass flows, and pulses of zinc-lead brine caused by tectonic activity. Build some layers. slide 26 lecture recording, 2 Oct

At this scale, does the ore look like veins that cut the rock, or layers in it? How thick are the pale bands?

Show what I see
  • A: a band of fine sphalerite grains parallel to the layering, with rounded clumps of sphalerite scattered in the darker sediment next to it.
  • B: pale sulphide bands, less than a millimetre thick, between dark carbonaceous mud layers. They curve gently over a lump.

So the sulphide is layered with the sediment; it does not cut it. There are very thin layers (microlaminae) of sulphide between layers of carbonaceous mudstone. The rounded clumps match sphalerite that grew in the water or brine and then sank. Notice how fine-grained everything is. This is why these ores are hard to process.

Not everyone agrees that all the sulphide flowed out onto the seafloor. Other models put it below the seafloor. One model is replacement of permeable layers soon after burial. Another is later tectonic replacement during deformation. At MacMillan Pass in the Yukon, Magnall and others suggested that barite formed first in the sediment and was later replaced by sulphide. Barite is barium sulphate, so incoming metals can react with it. They form sulphides and release barium, which is deposited again further away. At this deposit, sulphide veinlets that cut the layers, and sphalerite that replaces barite, support this model. But that is one small deposit. Most SEDEX deposits, and all the large ones, are best explained as exhalative and syngenetic. slides 27, 32 and 33 lecture recording, 2 Oct

Mineralising fluid that passes through a breccia near a vent will mineralise it too.

Exploration 117 to 130 m

North Australian SEDEX deposits and their halos

The SEDEX deposits of northern Australia have several features in common. They are next to major, deep faults that moved while the sediment was being deposited, and they are in dolomitic black shales. They are mostly distal, with no feeder zones or vent complexes. Many have several ore lenses. Pb/Zn changes both sideways and upwards, because each new brine pulse has a slightly different chemistry. Most are rich in zinc. slides 38 and 39 lecture recording, 2 Oct

Around them are large stratabound halos that you cannot see by eye. Ross Large and colleagues mapped iron-manganese carbonate halos at HYC, Century, Mount Isa and Lady Loretta. The carbonate in the host rock becomes richer in iron closer to the ore: dolomite → ferroan dolomite → → . Siderite appears as brown carbonate spots right around the ore body. Ankerite appears as pale orange or yellow carbonate further out. There are also Mn and thallium halos, a SEDEX alteration index, and carbon, oxygen and strontium isotope halos. Thallium may be only a few ppm, compared with a background of one ppm, so you can only see it with ICP-MS assays. slide 40 (Large et al., 1998) lecture recording, 2 Oct

The big picture 130 to 144 m

Where they are, how large they are, and how they were found

SEDEX deposits are found in only a few regions, because they need an unusual combination: a continental rift, an enclosed anoxic sea, and a change from permeable clastic rocks to impermeable shale and carbonate on top. Where you find one, there are usually several in the same basin. Northern Australia has more than anywhere else in the world. Others are in South Africa (Gamsberg, Broken Hill, Rosh Pinah, mostly strongly metamorphosed), Alaska (Red Dog), the Yukon (Howards Pass), British Columbia (Sullivan), Germany (Rammelsberg, mined since medieval times), Rajasthan in India, and western China. New South Wales has none, because it never had the right tectonic setting. slide 41 lecture recording, 2 Oct

Around Mount Isa and the Gulf there are HYC (McArthur River) in the Northern Territory, and Century, Mount Isa and Hilton-George Fisher, Dugald River, Lady Loretta and Cannington in Queensland. There are also smaller ones, like Walford Creek and Bluebush. Cannington, which the lecture groups with these, is Australia's largest silver mine. slide 42 lecture recording, 2 Oct

Large size also causes problems. Century was so large that its owners spent hundreds of millions of dollars on a slurry pipeline to the Gulf. A railway would have cost much more. Also, the far northwest floods in the wet season, which would stop a railway, and a mine this large must run 24 hours a day. Mount Isa already had a railway. lecture recording, 2 Oct

Grades are usually 5 to 20% Zn + Pb, and most deposits are less than 300 Mt, although some are much larger, like Red Dog. Some contain up to 2 wt% Cu, which is important at today's copper prices. By contained Zn + Pb, Hilton-George Fisher and Mount Isa together are the largest in the world, followed by Red Dog and HYC. Century was almost pure zinc, with very little lead.
