Rocks change after they form

After a rock forms, rain, oxygen and hot fluids continue to change it. These changes can hide ore, show where ore is, create ore, and sometimes cause problems for a mine. This lecture teaches you to read these changes. Start as you would in a core shed: look first.

Three processes

This lecture uses three words that are easy to confuse, so learn the difference now. is the physical and chemical breakdown of rock at or near the surface. It usually happens within a few tens of metres of the surface, because it needs air, water and often plants. is, in chemistry, the loss of electrons. In ore deposits it means that sulphide and carbonate minerals change into oxides and hydroxides when water with oxygen reaches them. is a change in a rock's minerals at low pressure, caused by fluids moving through the rock. It can happen anywhere the fluids go, at any scale, from a thin section to kilometres around a porphyry.

Why are these important? Each one changes what you see in the field. Weathered rock covers most of Australia and hides the geology below it. It rarely contains anything valuable, and it must be removed before open-cut mining. Oxidised rock can show where buried ore is (gossans) and where groundwater moved and collected. It can also be ore itself, for example iron ore, manganese ore, bauxite and the oxide parts of metal deposits. Alteration shows where hydrothermal fluid moved, often at the same time as metals were deposited. It also tells you the temperature, pH and mixing of the fluids. It is the most common tool for finding the direction to ore (a vector). But it can also turn hard rock into soft, weak material.

Concept 1 10 to 25 m

Weathering: breaking rock and dissolving it

Rock at the surface is destroyed in two ways, and they usually work together. Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its chemistry. Examples are gravity on steep slopes, wind-blown sand that scrapes the rock, plant roots that push cracks open, and rock expanding and shrinking as the temperature changes. Chemical weathering dissolves the minerals or changes them by chemical reactions. Water, climate and organic acids from dead plants drive it. In dry places physical weathering is stronger. In warm, wet places with many plants, chemical weathering is stronger.

Everything is frozen and there is no liquid water, so there is almost no chemical weathering.

What chemical weathering removes

This is the key idea, so read it slowly. Chemical weathering dissolves the first: sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, iron, metals, and often strontium. The rock that is left becomes richer in the : silicon, aluminium and titanium. Nothing is added. The immobile elements simply become a bigger part of a smaller amount of rock. This has two results. First, dark rocks become pale, because iron (the main colouring element) has been removed. This is called . Second, in the strongest tropical weathering, even silica is removed and only aluminium hydroxide is left. This is how forms. Bauxite is mostly white , often coloured pink or red by very fine hematite.

Worked example · residual enrichment, linked to L1

Imagine a basalt that starts with 15 wt% Al2O3. Chemical weathering dissolves and removes 60 per cent of the rock's mass, but all the aluminium stays.

Start: 100 g rock contains 15 g Al2O3 After: 40 g rock still contains 15 g Al2O3 Grade = 15 / 40 = 37.5 wt% Al2O3 (concentration factor 2.5)

You have concentrated aluminium 2.5 times without adding any aluminium. If you also remove the silica, you reach bauxite grades. This is the same concentration factor idea from Lecture 1. The difference is that the concentration happens by removing other elements, not by adding metal.

What controls the speed and depth of weathering

Five things control it, and you can test each one in the machine above. Composition and grain size: soluble minerals (halides, carbonates, sulphates, sulphides) weather fastest. Coarse-grained rocks weather faster than fine-grained rocks, because water can enter along the long boundaries of each big grain. Structure: bedding, joints, fractures, foliation, faults, shears and rock contacts all let water in. This is also why you rarely see a clear geological contact in the field: contacts are weak planes, so they weather first. Topography: steep relief means more physical weathering. Climate: heat makes reactions faster, liquid water is essential, large temperature changes between day and night crack rock (in the desert you can sometimes hear it), and wind wears rock away. Vegetation: thick plant cover keeps the ground wet and releases organic acids. So warm, humid forests have the deepest weathering. Different plants produce different acids, and scientists are still studying this.

Field note · acid rain and cavesslide 11 and lecture recording, 15 Sep

Karst (caves and sinkholes in limestone) is the best example of a landscape made by chemical weathering. Weakly acidic rain and groundwater dissolve calcite. Rain has become more acidic since the industrial revolution, so karst has probably been dissolving faster. In one groundwater study in north Queensland, smoke from fires used to clear land in Indonesia and Malaysia lowered the rainwater pH to below 3. Below the water table, almost no weathering happens. But water tables move up and down all the time, so the zone of weathering also moves.

Weathered rock looks strange until you know what to look for. Do these tasks as you would on a field trip: describe first, then interpret.

Field task · Boundary South pit, Manuka mineslide 19

Describe this pit wall from top to bottom. What are the layers, and what did water do to make them?

Show what I see

  • Middle: a pale, bleached zone. You can still see bedding, but the iron has gone.
  • Bottom: brown, iron-altered limestone, and then fresh rock.

The water table moved up and down through this profile many times. Each time, it carried dissolved iron. It removed iron from the bleached zone and deposited it above and below. Iron is the element that groundwater moves in the most visible way. Also notice that the iron is not spread evenly: a fault through the pit controls where the high-iron bands are.

Concept 3 35 to 50 m

Oxidation: why sulphides rust and make acid

Oxidation is the loss of electrons, and reduction is the gain of electrons. For ore deposits, the main idea is simple. Sulphides form deep underground, in reduced conditions with no free oxygen. When they come close to the surface and meet water with oxygen, they are no longer stable. They react: the iron becomes oxides and hydroxides, and the sulphur becomes sulphate. The minerals that oxidise most often are the iron minerals: pyrite (FeS2), pyrrhotite (Fe1-xS) and the carbonate siderite (FeCO3).

A useful rule: hematite means flowing water and plenty of free oxygen. Goethite contains hydrogen, so it needs water that stays in one place. It shows where water collected, for example at a water table. So the iron oxides in a profile are a map of old groundwater. slide 23 and lecture recording

What controls oxidation

Four things control oxidation. (1) A supply of oxygenated water, which is water with dissolved oxygen. Without it, nothing happens. (2) Minerals that can oxidise, such as sulphides and carbonates. (3) Structure, porosity and permeability, such as fractures, bedding, joints, foliation and faults. (4) Climate: rainfall, temperature, and especially a water table that moves up and down. slide 26

Concept 4 50 to 60 m

Gossans: the rusty cap on a sulphide body

A is what is left of a sulphide body at the surface after oxidation. It is rich in iron and is made mostly of goethite, hematite and ferrihydrite. It also contains secondary minerals made by the oxidation, but only a little of the valuable sulphides (galena, sphalerite, chalcopyrite). Gossans are in the oxidised zone above the water table. They were the first ores that humans mined, because they are at the surface and easy to see. A gossan can show that a deposit is below it, but not always.

Field task · an unknown gossan, southern Cobar fieldslide 28

No prospector or miner ever recorded this outcrop. Later, geologists found visible gold all through it, but no sulphide was left at all. Why did the gold survive when everything else oxidised?

Show the reasoning

Gold does not oxidise. The pyrite or pyrrhotite that once contained the gold changed completely to goethite. But the gold grains stayed. They even became relatively richer, because the rock around them lost mass. Two lessons: there are still undiscovered gossans, even in well-explored districts like Cobar. And you must assay a gossan sample for metals; you cannot judge it by how it looks.

A gossan is a good sign, but it does not always mean that there is an economic ore deposit below it. Most of a gossan comes from oxidised pyrite, with much less of the valuable sulphides. slide 27 In the lab, look for a texture: the Pyrite Hill specimens include cellular boxwork gossan made of goethite and quartz. lab specimen sheet

Concept 5 60 to 75 m

The supergene profile: where copper becomes richer

Now combine these ideas into the most important picture in this lecture. Start with a primary copper sulphide body (chalcopyrite) and let rain soak into it for a long time. Above the water table, conditions are oxidising. Below the water table, conditions are reducing. Copper dissolves in the oxidising zone and moves down with the water. At the water table it meets reducing conditions and is deposited again as copper-rich sulphides. The water table is the boundary between oxidation and reduction, so metals collect there. This richest layer is the zone.

From top to bottom the profile has: a gossan; a leached zone where the metal has been removed; an oxide zone with brightly coloured copper minerals (malachite, azurite, cuprite, chrysocolla, native copper); the enrichment blanket at the water table, with chalcocite, covellite and bornite; and below that, the unaltered primary chalcopyrite. Real deposit · Girilambone, NSWslide 29

At Girilambone, only the supergene chalcocite (Cu2S) blanket was worth mining. The oxide zone above it and the primary sulphide below it were both too low grade. Supergene zones are usually only a few metres thick. Some deposits have none, and some have up to about ten metres. So the blanket can decide whether a project succeeds or fails.

Field note · why modern mines do not want native copperlecture recording, 15 Sep

Native copper sounds like great ore, but in a modern mine it causes problems. It is soft and malleable, so it does not break in the crusher. Instead it spreads out and blocks the crusher. So mines put it in a separate stockpile. Heap leaching also works badly with it, because pure copper uses up a lot of acid. The usual solution is to mix it slowly with lower-grade ore.

Real deposit · Broken Hill, NSWslide 31

Most oxide zones are only a few metres thick. But if there are several weathering periods, millions of years apart, the oxide zone can become more than a hundred metres thick. Broken Hill had at least three weathering periods. Its oxide zone was extremely rich, with masses of native silver and chlorargyrite (AgCl) that weighed more than a tonne. Concept 6 75 to 90 m

Deep oxidation: faults can take oxygen deep

Oxidation normally stops at the water table. A fault can change this. Water with oxygen can flow down a fault plane for millions of years. It can oxidise rock hundreds of metres below the normal oxide zone. So in one drill hole you can pass through fresh sulphide and then reach oxidised ore again.

Real deposit · Tsumeb, Namibiaslides 32 to 36

Tsumeb is a pipe-shaped orebody in limestone and dolostone. It had a normal oxide zone from the surface down to Level 11. Below that was fresh sulphide. Then there was a second oxide zone from Level 24 to Level 35, hundreds of metres deep. It formed because a fault zone let water with oxygen flow down for millions of years. Because of this deep oxidation, Tsumeb produced tens of thousands of world-class specimens of secondary minerals.

Concept 7 90 to 100 m

Alteration: fluids change the rock

Alteration is a change in a rock's minerals at low pressure. It is caused by fluids moving through the rock, or by oxygen. Hot hydrothermal fluid, which is often acidic, reacts with the minerals it passes. It adds some elements and removes others. Feldspars change to clays or micas. Ferromagnesian minerals change to chlorite and epidote. The rock can gain silica, potassium or sulphur, and lose sodium, calcium or iron. Alteration happens at every scale, from microns to kilometres. Around ore deposits, it can be one metre wide next to a vein, or about two kilometres wide around a porphyry. This is why alteration is the most common vector to ore: the alteration halo is much bigger than the ore.

Four kinds of alteration

MineralogicalNew minerals that you can see: phyllic, propylitic, argillic, kaolinisation, hematisation.
Mineral structuralThe mineral is the same, but its crystal structure changes, for example illite or muscovite crystallinity.
Geochemical (cryptic)No visible change, only a change in chemistry: for example barium around VHMS systems, or rare earths in some sediment-hosted deposits.
IsotopicChanged isotope ratios in carbonates and sulphides. This is important around Carlin-type gold deposits.
Field note · the gold lode that cannot be minedlecture recording, 15 Sep

At the Hera mine in the Cobar Basin, there is a gold lode worth tens of millions of dollars that will probably never be mined. Nobody checked the mineralogy before they dug a tunnel towards it. The host rock had strong chlorite and talc alteration. It was much too weak to support underground tunnels. So alteration does not only guide you to ore. It also decides whether you can mine the ore safely. Always study the mineralogy first.

Why alteration matters

Alteration shows that hydrothermal fluid moved through the rock. It often happened at the same time as metals were deposited. It records the temperature, pH and pressure of the fluid. It can show where different waters mixed. For example, cool, neutral rainwater may meet hot, acidic hydrothermal fluid, and in epithermal systems this often causes gold and silver to be deposited. And alteration can make the host rock much weaker, as the Hera example shows.

What controls where alteration happens and how strong it is

Where alteration happens depends on several things. Primary controls are the composition of the host rock, its structure, porosity and permeability, how reactive the rock is, and how complex the geology is. Secondary controls are later faults and fractures, and secondary porosity. The fluid itself also matters (its pH, composition and volume), and so does overprinting by later fluids. How strong the alteration is depends mainly on three things. First, how reactive the host rock is: rock with carbonate reacts much more than rock rich in quartz. Second, permeability, especially secondary permeability from fracturing. Third, the volume and type of fluid. A low-pH fluid attacks rock much more strongly. slides 49 and 50

Zoning: how alteration maps a hydrothermal system

Around a hydrothermal system, alteration forms zones. This is because the temperature and the fluid chemistry change with distance from the source. If you can recognise the zones, you can tell which direction the centre of the system is. You need to know two classic models in detail.

Remember this pattern. A potassic core (quartz, K-feldspar, biotite) sits above the unaltered porphyry. Inside the potassic zone there is a low-grade core, which is usually not mined. The ore shell (1 to 3 per cent chalcopyrite, with the gold) is around the top of the intrusion (the cupola), at the edge of the potassic zone. Next is a phyllic zone (quartz, sericite, pyrite). Advanced argillic alteration is at the top. There are also distal copper-lead-zinc-silver-gold veins out in the propylitic zone. VMS (VHMS)

On the seafloor, most of the alteration is in the footwall, because the hanging wall rocks were deposited after the ore. Under the massive sulphide there is a feeder pipe. Its core is very iron-rich chlorite and sericite with thin sulphide veins (stringers). These stringers usually contain chalcopyrite, not lead or zinc, and are sometimes rich enough to mine. Directly under the massive sulphide there is quartz with iron-rich chlorite. Around the outside there is magnesium-rich chlorite. So the chlorite composition is a vector you can see: iron-rich chlorite is dark green, and magnesium-rich chlorite is almost colourless. Carbonates show the same pattern: Mg-rich far away, Fe-rich near the ore. Barium is highest above the ore. Manganese and iron oxide deposits and barite form on the seafloor away from the vents. So if you find barite, you are in the right system, but still far from the ore. slides 58 and 61, lecture recording 18 Sep

Concept 10 125 to 140 m

Measuring alteration: from core to numbers

Your eyes are the first tool, but they are not enough. A proper alteration study works at every scale, from the deposit to the microscope. Thin sections show the mineral groups. Most importantly, they show overprinting relationships, which tell you which alteration came first. identifies fine-grained clays and micas that you cannot see clearly under the microscope. Quantitative XRD can even measure how much illite and how much muscovite there is, and how crystalline the illite is. Spectroscopy identifies clays and chlorites quickly. For cryptic alteration, you need chemistry: ICP-MS and XRF in the lab, and in the field.

Field note · what pXRF can and cannot dolecture recording, 18 Sep

Mining companies use pXRF all the time. It is excellent for copper, lead and zinc. It is poor for rare earths, not very good for silver unless there is a lot of it, and useless for gold. Know the weaknesses of your instrument before you trust its numbers.

You can also date alteration directly. You can use K-Ar or Ar-Ar dating on illite, muscovite or adularia, and now also U-Pb dating on epidote-group minerals. If the alteration formed at the same time as the mineralisation, this gives the age of the ore-forming event, not just the age of the host rock.

For VMS systems, there is a simple way to measure how strong the alteration is, using only whole-rock chemistry. The (AI) measures how much feldspar and volcanic glass have been destroyed and replaced by sericite and chlorite:

AI = 100 × (K2O + MgO) / (K2O + MgO + Na2O + CaO) Ishikawa et al. 1976 CCPI = 100 × (MgO + FeO) / (MgO + FeO + Na2O + K2O) Large et al. 2001

The (CCPI) measures how much chlorite, Fe-Mg carbonate and pyrite have grown. Plot the two indices against each other. Altered rocks move up and to the right on the plot. Load the real Cowley Hills samples, and then change the oxide values yourself.

Down the drill hole, AI and CCPI fall a little and then rise sharply at the mineralisation. After the mineralisation they fall again. Sodium shows the opposite pattern. Also, if a unit that should be distal plots in the "favourable" footwall field, check it again. You may have interpreted it wrongly, and there may be undiscovered mineralisation nearby.

Immobile elements: seeing through alteration

Sometimes a rock is so altered that you cannot tell what it was. Then you use the elements that fluids cannot move: zirconium, titanium, yttrium, chromium and aluminium. The ratios of these elements do not change during alteration. So plots such as TiO2 against Zr, or immobile against mobile elements (TiO2 vs SiO2, Zr vs K2O), show the original rock. At Cowley Hills, some dolerites had been mapped as much younger than the volcanic rocks. But on Ti-Zr and Y-Zr plots they plot in the same place as the basalts. So they came from the same magma and are the same age (coeval). No dating was needed. slide 72 and lecture recording, 18 Sep

A related method is . It converts mobile oxides (Na, K, Ca) to molar ratios and plots them against an element that does not move, usually aluminium. Unaltered rocks plot on the feldspar lines. As alteration removes Na, K and Ca, the samples move towards kaolinite. In one Indonesian epithermal system, the barren footwall plotted on the plagioclase line. The mineralised hanging wall units had moved far towards kaolinite. slide 55

Vectoring to ore: Hera, veins and isotopes

The most important question for an explorer is: how far away is the ore? At the Hera deposit in the Cobar Basin, the company drilled a 1.5 km hole to study what the rock layers could tell them. Why does this work? Strontium is very mobile in hydrothermal fluid, so the fluid removes it. Dividing by titanium, which is immobile, removes the effect of changes in rock type. Vein deposits: almost no halo

Field task · narrow alteration around veinsslides 69 and 70
A · Sn-W vein
B · bleaching
C · silver ore

Why can you not use a large alteration halo to find veins like A? What does the pale rock in B and C tell you?

Show what I see

Vein A contains a lot of tin and tungsten, but its alteration zone is only a few centimetres wide. Hydrothermal vein deposits usually have very narrow alteration zones, one metre wide at most. So alteration cannot guide you to them from far away. But you do see bleaching. Acidic fluid removes iron and magnesium, and the silicon and aluminium that stay make the rock pale. Nothing was added; the rock is white because the colouring elements have gone. When you start to see bleaching like B, you are close to the vein. In C, the dark ore contains about 2000 ppm silver, and the host rock fragments inside it are completely bleached.

Isotopes can also point towards ore. In one regional study in northern NSW, sulphur isotopes in sphalerite, arsenopyrite, galena and pyrite changed steadily towards the more magmatic, tin-bearing part of the system. The values were around zero in the silver veins and negative towards the tin. So the isotopes showed the direction of the heat source, and which metal you were likely to find. lecture recording, 18 Sep
