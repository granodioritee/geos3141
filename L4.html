Learning to see in shades of grey

Most ore minerals are opaque, so the transmitted-light petrography you learned in second year shows them as black blobs. Bounce light off a polished surface instead and a whole world opens up: subtle colours, brightness, crystal shapes, and the order minerals grew in. This is ore microscopy, also called mineragraphy, and it decides how a deposit is mined and processed. Start with the money.

Why ore microscopy matters

Assays tell you how much metal is in a rock. They don't tell you where it is, and that turns out to matter just as much. Five questions only the microscope answers well:

Which mineral hosts the metal?Free gold can be recovered cheaply by gravity. gold locked in pyrite or arsenopyrite needs roasting, pressure oxidation or bacterial leaching first, which can add hundreds of millions of dollars to a plant.
How big are the grains?Grain size sets how finely you have to grind to liberate the ore mineral, and grinding is one of the biggest costs in processing.
Any dirty minerals?Arsenic- and tellurium-bearing phases attract smelter penalties and can be environmental hazards. Better to find them before you sell concentrate.
Which alteration goes with ore?If a particular alteration mineral always sits with the ore, that's an exploration vector.

The reflected-light microscope

It's almost exactly the petrological microscope you already know, with one change. Instead of light coming up through a thin section from below, it comes down through the objective from a side illuminator and reflects off the polished surface back up to your eye. Why bother? Ore minerals are opaque in transmitted light because their dense, heavy-element structures absorb it. A reflective surface turns that problem into information. You can use polished thin sections, polished blocks or polished grain mounts. slide 11

The screen in B looks almost entirely grey. What's the single biggest practical difficulty for beginners in reflected light?

Show the answer

Everything is a shade of grey, white or pale yellow, and the differences are subtle. You get used to it. Two tips: ideally work in a dark room so your eye adapts (old-school microscopists used a completely blacked-out room), and always compare a grain with its neighbours, because perceived brightness and colour depend on what's next to it. Pyrite looks bright beside sphalerite and noticeably duller beside galena.

The properties you can see

Identification in reflected light works by combining a handful of properties. None is enough alone; together they narrow things down fast.

ColourPlane-polarised light (PPL), one polar inBody colour and its intensity: bright white, pale yellow, pinkish brown, blue, grey with a brown tint.
ReflectancePPLHow bright the grain is relative to its neighbours: high, moderate or low. Always judge relative to what's around it.

Field task · covellite and sphaleriteslides 7 and 8

The first two are the same covellite grains, rotated 45°. Name the property. The third is sphalerite under crossed polars. Where are those coloured spots coming from?

Show what I see

Covellite shows extreme bireflectance pleochroism: rotate the stage and the blue changes in both tone and brightness. Nothing else common looks like this, so once you've seen it you won't mistake covellite. The sphalerite shows internal reflections: light refracts into the semi-transparent grain, bounces off fractures and comes back as yellow, orange and red flashes. In sphalerite that's diagnostic.

Memorise
  • Work in order: colour, reflectance, anisotropy, polishing hardness, internal reflections.
  • Isotropic stays dark under crossed polars; anisotropic changes on rotation.

Concept 5 76 to 88 m

Tricky pairs, and how to split them

Most mistakes come from a few look-alike groups. Learn the one property that splits each group.

Three yellowsPyrite is pale yellow, hard and takes a perfect polish. Chalcopyrite is a stronger yellow and soft. Gold is yellow-orange and so bright it still glows with the lamp turned right down, and it polishes poorly.
Pyrite vs marcasiteBoth FeS2. Marcasite is more bronze-yellow and strongly anisotropic in blue and yellow-green. It also breaks down in warm, humid air, even in Sydney.
Galena vs arsenopyriteBoth white. Galena is soft, isotropic and covered in triangular cleavage pits. Arsenopyrite is hard, anisotropic and forms sharp rhomb-shaped crystals.
Low-reflectance greysMagnetite has a brown tint, is hard, isotropic and magnetic. Sphalerite gives internal reflections. Chromite is brownish black with curved conchoidal fractures, usually with olivine; Al-rich chromite is reddish brown.
Pentlandite vs chalcopyritePentlandite is a paler yellow, isotropic, and almost always sits with pyrrhotite. That association is your best clue.
The two hardestChalcocite is featureless grey-white, soft and covered in scratches, with a bluish tarnish. Tetrahedrite is grey with a brownish tint, isotropic and moderately hard; silver-rich ones look bluer. Expect to struggle with both.

Concept 6 88 to 106 m

Micro-textures: the order things grew

There are dozens of named micro-textures (bladed, botryoidal, colloform, framboidal, skeletal, poikilitic, atoll, and on and on). Don't worry about naming them all; describe what you see. What matters is what a texture says about timing. Some are obvious, like a vein cutting another mineral. Many are subtle, and the main mineralising event can be distinct from, and even unrelated to, earlier and later ones. So never build a paragenesis from one sample: look at as many as you can, and link what you see at every scale from mine to microscope. slides 9 and 15

Field note · two textures with surprising historieslecture recording, 21 Sep

Chalcopyrite disease is a dusting of tiny chalcopyrite blebs inside sphalerite. Older literature (before about 1980) read it as exsolution. Modern work shows it's usually chalcopyrite replacing sphalerite along crystallographic directions when hotter, copper-bearing fluid came through after the sphalerite formed. Framboids (from the French for raspberry) are spherical clusters of tiny pyrite crystals. They were long taken as proof of microbial sulphate reduction, and in black shale they usually are, but framboids also form in hydrothermal systems, and galena can make them too. A texture is a clue, not a verdict.

At the lab test
  • Chalcopyrite disease: tiny chalcopyrite blebs replacing sphalerite.
  • Framboids: spherical clusters of tiny pyrite, typical of black shale.
  • Euhedral doesn’t mean early: porphyroblasts can grow last and engulf their neighbours.
  • A fracture fill is younger than the mineral it cuts.

Building a paragenesis

A is the crystallisation order of the minerals in a deposit, usually drawn as a chart of bars against time. You build it by collecting timing clues from many textures and stitching them into one consistent sequence. Here are four clues from one hypothetical Cobar-style deposit. Put the minerals in order, oldest at the top.

Paragenesis puzzlearrange the minerals

  • Euhedral pyrite cubes have caries embayments filled by sphalerite.
  • Sphalerite contains chalcopyrite disease.
  • Chalcopyrite and sphalerite are both cut by thin galena veinlets.
  • Galena encloses smooth, cuspate-lobate blebs of tetrahedrite with no cross-cutting either way.
