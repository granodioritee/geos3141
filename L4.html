Identifying minerals in shades of grey

Most ore minerals are opaque. In the transmitted-light microscope from second year, they just look black. But if light reflects off a polished surface, you can see much more: small colour differences, brightness, crystal shapes, and the order in which minerals grew. This is ore microscopy, also called mineragraphy. It affects how a deposit is mined and processed. We start with an example about money.

Why ore microscopy matters

Assays tell you how much metal is in a rock. They do not tell you where the metal is, and this is just as important. The microscope is the best tool for answering five questions:

Which mineral contains the metal?Free gold can be recovered cheaply by gravity. gold is locked inside pyrite or arsenopyrite as tiny grains, and it is harder and more expensive to recover. This matters for exploration, mining and processing.
How big are the grains?Grain size decides how finely you must grind the ore to free (liberate) the ore mineral. Grinding is one of the biggest costs in processing.
Are there any unwanted minerals?Minerals that contain arsenic or tellurium cause penalties at the smelter and can be dangerous to the environment. It is better to find them before you sell the concentrate.
Which alteration occurs with the ore?If a certain alteration mineral is always found with the ore, you can use it as an exploration vector.
In what order did the minerals grow?The relationships between ore, gangue and host rock record the history of the deposit. This history is its .

The reflected-light microscope

It is almost the same as the petrological microscope you already know, with one difference. In a normal microscope, light comes up through a thin section from below. In a reflected-light microscope, light comes from a lamp at the side, goes down through the objective, and reflects off the polished surface back up to your eye. Why is this useful? Ore minerals are opaque in transmitted light, because their dense structures with heavy elements absorb the light. But their reflective surfaces give you useful information. You can use polished thin sections, polished blocks or polished grain mounts. slide 11

Everything is a shade of grey, white or pale yellow, and the differences are small. You will get used to it. One tip: always compare a grain with the grains next to it, because how bright a grain looks and what colour it seems depend on its neighbours. Pyrite looks bright next to sphalerite, but clearly duller next to galena.

The properties you can see

To identify a mineral in reflected light, you combine several properties. One property alone is not enough. Together, they quickly reduce the number of possible minerals.

PropertyWhat you doWhat you look for
ColourPlane-polarised light (PPL), one polar inThe colour of the mineral and how strong it is: bright white, pale yellow, pinkish brown, blue, grey with a brown tint.
ReflectancePPLHow bright the grain is compared with the grains around it: high, moderate or low. Always compare it with its neighbours.
Rotate the stage in PPLThe brightness (not the colour) changes as you rotate.
Bireflectance pleochroismRotate the stage in PPLThe colour changes as you rotate. Covellite shows this most strongly.
Cross the polars, rotate grains stay black at every angle (galena). Anisotropic grains show colours and go dark four times in one rotation (covellite). All cubic minerals are isotropic, but pyrite is a well-known exception.
Crossed polars, often high powerLight enters the grain, reflects off fractures inside it, and comes back out. Sphalerite is the classic example. Hematite and wolframite give deep red internal reflections.
Polishing hardnessLook at the grain edgesHard grains stand higher, with sharp edges, and polish well. Soft grains have scratches and pits.
Field task · covellite and sphaleriteslides 7 and 8

The first two photos show the same covellite grains, rotated 45°. Name the property. The third photo is sphalerite under crossed polars. Where do the coloured spots come from?

Show what I see

Covellite shows very strong bireflectance pleochroism: when you rotate the stage, the blue changes in both colour and brightness. No other common mineral looks like this, so once you have seen it, you will always recognise covellite. The sphalerite shows internal reflections. Light enters the partly transparent grain, reflects off fractures inside it, and comes back as yellow, orange and red flashes. In sphalerite, this property is diagnostic.

Memorise
  • Work in order: colour, reflectance, anisotropy, polishing hardness, internal reflections.
  • Isotropic stays dark under crossed polars; anisotropic changes on rotation.

Concept 5 76 to 88 m

Minerals that look alike, and how to tell them apart

Most mistakes happen with a few groups of minerals that look alike. For each group, learn the one property that separates them.

Three yellow mineralsPyrite is pale yellow, hard and polishes perfectly. Chalcopyrite is a stronger yellow and soft. Gold is yellow-orange and very bright: it still shines when the lamp is turned down low. Gold polishes poorly.
Pyrite vs marcasiteBoth are FeS2. Marcasite is more bronze-yellow and strongly anisotropic in blue and yellow-green. It also breaks down in warm, humid air, even in Sydney.
Galena vs arsenopyriteBoth are white. Galena is soft, isotropic and covered in triangular cleavage pits. Arsenopyrite is hard and anisotropic, and forms sharp rhomb-shaped crystals.
Grey minerals with low reflectanceMagnetite has a brown tint, is hard and isotropic, and is magnetic. Sphalerite has internal reflections. Chromite is brownish black with curved conchoidal fractures, and usually occurs with olivine. Al-rich chromite is reddish brown.
Pentlandite vs chalcopyritePentlandite is a paler yellow and isotropic, and it almost always occurs with pyrrhotite. This association is the best clue.
The two hardest to identifyChalcocite is plain grey-white and soft, with many scratches and a bluish tarnish. Tetrahedrite is grey with a brownish tint, isotropic and moderately hard. Silver-rich tetrahedrite looks bluer. Most students find these two difficult.

Concept 6 88 to 106 m

Micro-textures: the order in which minerals grew

There are many named micro-textures, for example bladed, botryoidal, colloform, framboidal, skeletal, poikilitic and atoll. You do not need to know all the names. Describe what you see. The most important thing is what a texture tells you about timing. Some textures are easy to read, such as a vein that cuts another mineral. Many are harder. Also, the main mineralising event can be separate from earlier and later events, and even unrelated to them. So never build a paragenesis from only one sample. Look at as many samples as you can, and connect what you see at every scale, from the mine to the microscope. slides 9 and 15

Field note · two textures that are easy to misreadlecture recording, 21 Sep

Chalcopyrite disease is many tiny chalcopyrite spots inside sphalerite. Older studies (before about 1980) said it formed by exsolution. Modern studies show that it usually forms when chalcopyrite replaces sphalerite along crystal directions. This happens when hotter fluid with copper flows through after the sphalerite has formed. Framboids (from the French word for raspberry) are round clusters of tiny pyrite crystals. For a long time, people thought they always formed by microbes reducing sulphate. In black shale this is usually true. But framboids also form in hydrothermal systems, and galena can form them too. A texture is a clue, not a final answer.

At the lab test
  • Chalcopyrite disease: tiny chalcopyrite blebs replacing sphalerite.
  • Framboids: spherical clusters of tiny pyrite, typical of black shale.
  • Euhedral doesn’t mean early: porphyroblasts can grow last and engulf their neighbours.
  • A fracture fill is younger than the mineral it cuts.

Building a paragenesis

A is the order in which the minerals in a deposit crystallised. It is usually drawn as a chart with bars against time. To build one, you collect timing clues from many textures and combine them into one sequence that fits all the clues. Here are four clues from one imaginary Cobar-style deposit. Put the minerals in order, with the oldest at the top.

Paragenesis puzzlearrange the minerals

  • Euhedral pyrite cubes have caries holes filled by sphalerite.
  • Sphalerite contains chalcopyrite disease.
  • Thin galena veinlets cut both chalcopyrite and sphalerite.
  • Galena surrounds smooth, cuspate-lobate spots of tetrahedrite. Neither mineral cuts the other.
