Reading a hand specimen

You need three things: a hand lens (10 to 20 times is fine), a hardness kit and a streak plate. Then use your eyes before anything else. hand specimen identification notes, page 1

Lustre first. Is it metallic or not? That one call halves the list. Non-metallic lustres have names worth learning by example: vitreous (quartz), sub-vitreous (plagioclase), pearly (calcite), dull (kaolinite), resinous (sulphur) and adamantine (sphalerite). GEOS2181 Labs 1-2, slides 12 and 13

Then streak, not colour. Colour lies: fluorite and calcite come in every colour, but the powder is always colourless or white. Most silicates streak white. Streak earns its keep with the iron oxides: goethite streaks yellow-brown, hematite red-brown, magnetite brown-black. The plate has to be harder than the mineral, or you just scratch the plate. GEOS2181 Labs 1-2, slides 8 and 9

Then hardness, with what’s in your pocket. Mohs is relative: diamond (10) is about five times harder than corundum (9). In the lab you bracket a mineral between tools. GEOS2181 Labs 1-2, slides 28 and 29

Then cleavage, heft and the odd tests. Count the cleavage planes and the angle between them: micas have one perfect basal cleavage, feldspars and pyroxenes two at about 90°, amphiboles two at 60° and 120°, calcite three at 60°. Quartz, olivine and garnet have none, just fracture (conchoidal in quartz). Heft the specimen: quartz is 2.7, galena 7.6, gold 15 to 19. Finally the specials: magnetite is strongly magnetic and pyrrhotite moderately; talc feels soapy; clays smell earthy; struck sulphides smell of rotten eggs; heated arsenic minerals smell of garlic; molybdenite bends and stays bent; mica bends and springs back. GEOS2181 Labs 1-2, slides 22 to 33 hand specimen identification notes, pages 4 to 6

At the lab test
  • Work in the same order every time: lustre, streak, hardness, cleavage, heft, then any special test. Write each result down before you name the mineral.
  • Use streak to split the iron oxides (yellow-brown goethite, red-brown hematite, black magnetite) and a magnet to confirm magnetite.
  • Use hardness to split the brassy sulphides: pyrite is harder than steel, chalcopyrite is not.

The game

The Sudoku

Each card is one row of the lab’s sheet, with the lab’s own hand specimens of that mineral: flick through them and look for the colour, lustre, cleavage and habit before you answer. Plain values are given on the sheet. Tap a ? to fill it: four choices, one right. First try pays an ounce and builds the combo; finishing a card pays a bonus.

Name that mineral

Like a lab station: you see the specimen and one test result (two when there’s no photo). Name the mineral, or ask for another clue (each costs an ounce). Colour comes last, because it’s the least reliable clue you have. Ten minerals per game.

Where the values come from

The minerals, specimen codes and every plain value are exactly what the GEOS3141 Minerals Sudoku 2024 fill-in sheet gives. Blanks marked notes come from the course’s own notes: the GEOS2181 Labs 1-2 deck and the hand specimen identification notes. Blanks marked added are standard mineralogy values added for this site, not from the course, so treat them as a guide and trust the real specimens. Where a sheet value disagrees with the course notes or standard references (quartz’s density, chalcocite’s formula and a few others), the card keeps the sheet’s value and flags it.

Photos are from the lab’s hand-specimen set (Moodle book “Lab Material - minerals in hand specimen”, photos by Ian Graham on the course Flickr account). Fill in your own paper sheet too: this page is practice for the bench, not a copy to hand in.
