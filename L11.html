GEOS3141 Mineral and Energy Resources · Lecture 11 · T3 2026

A billion tonnes of low-grade ore

Porphyry deposits are the world's main source of copper and molybdenum, and an important source of gold. But their ore has, on average, much less than 1% copper. They are profitable because they are very large. A porphyry deposit is a few intrusions in the root of an arc volcano. The rock is fractured and soaked by fluid from the intrusions, and it is zoned in shells that you can map from the surface. If you learn the shells, you can find the ore. Try it now.

What a porphyry deposit is

A deposit is a deposit. Fluid from a cooling intrusion carries metals out of it and deposits them in and around it. Like any deposit, it needs five things: a source of metals, a way to carry them, a way to concentrate them, a way to deposit them, and a later history that preserves them. slide 2

The porphyry is in the centre of a much bigger system. A few kilometres above are epithermal veins (Lecture 14). In limestone next to the intrusion, Cu-Au-Fe skarns form. Further out, Pb-Zn-Ag skarns and veins form, sometimes tens of kilometres away. All of these come from the same magma. slide 3 (after Corbett, 2008) lecture recording, 9 Oct

Porphyry Cu-Au deposits are by far the world's largest producer and resource of copper, and an important source of gold. Porphyry Mo deposits are the world's largest source of molybdenum. Many are larger than 500 Mt, and some are larger than 1000 Mt. But the grades are low: usually 0.1 to 0.5% Cu, about 0.3 g/t Au and up to about 0.5% Mo. All of them have typical alteration zones over a large area, which makes them easier to explore for than you might expect. slide 5

Low grade only works at a very large scale. A new porphyry mine on the border of Argentina and Chile will cost about $10 billion before any rock is mined, including a 150 km railway. Companies like BHP and Rio Tinto accept this, because a porphyry mine runs for decades. Over that time, the ups and downs of metal prices balance out. lecture recording, 9 Oct

Memorise
  • Porphyry Cu-Au: the world’s largest source of Cu, significant Au. Porphyry Mo: the largest source of Mo.
  • Huge (many >500 Mt, some >1000 Mt) and low grade (0.1 to 0.5% Cu, ~0.3 g/t Au).
  • Distinctive regional-scale alteration zoning.

The numbers 13 to 26 m

Size, grade and the largest deposits

Many of the largest deposits (super-giants) are actually several mineralised stocks close together. Rio Blanco-Los Bronces and Escondida are groups like this. Bingham (30 Mt Cu) and Resolution (24 Mt Cu) are exceptions: they are single zoned porphyries. There is no relationship between the volume of a porphyry and the amount of copper in it. What matters is the earliest porphyry phase, which contains most of the metal. Later phases and fluids only dilute it. slide 8

In some places the grade can be higher. Magmatic-hydrothermal breccias focus Cu-rich fluid and can contain more than 1% Cu. Half of the mineralisation at Rio Blanco-Los Bronces is in breccia. Ideally, the copper arrives early, with high-temperature potassic alteration. Later sericite or chlorite-sericite fluids can remove it again. Skarns next to porphyries can also be large: Antamina in Peru has 17 Mt Cu. slide 9

Where the copper is

Contained Cu from slides 6 and 7. Producers and reserves (USGS, thousand tonnes) from slide 14.

Porphyries were found in the 19th century, but their grade was too low to mine. Large-scale mining at Bingham Canyon started in 1906. At first it mined the rich supergene blanket (chalcocite and covellite, up to four times the primary grade). The primary ore was mined decades later. Today, more than 50% of the world's copper comes from about 100 porphyry mines. They use very large open pits or underground block caving, as at Oyu Tolgoi in Mongolia. slides 11, 12 and 23 lecture recording, 9 Oct

Australia produced about 730 kt of copper in 2025, much less than Chile's 5300 kt. But Australia has the second largest reserves (100 000 kt). Most of the large producers mine porphyries. The Congo and Zambia are the exception: they mine sediment-hosted copper. slide 14 lecture recording, 9 Oct

Concept 1 26 to 39 m

Arcs, slabs and oxidised magma

Porphyries form in magmatic arcs above subduction zones. They form in lines parallel to the volcanoes, and they are slightly older than the volcanoes. The subducting oceanic crust contains water in its minerals. As it heats up, amphibole and serpentine break down and release fluid into the mantle wedge above. This fluid is rich in Cl, S, large-ion lithophile elements (Rb, K, Cs, Ba, Sr), Li, B, Pb, As, Sb, Cu and Au, and it is oxidising (2FeO + H₂O = Fe₂O₃ + H₂). It lowers the melting point of the wedge, which partly melts. slides 17 and 18 lecture recording, 9 Oct

The basalt melt collects at the base of the crust in a . MASH means: Melting of crustal rocks, Assimilation, Storage and Homogenisation. Here it becomes a mixed calc-alkaline magma, and the porphyry magmas come from this zone. The most important property is oxidation. Porphyry Cu-Au magmas are near the hematite-magnetite buffer, at 400 to 800 °C or more. Porphyry Sn-W magmas are reduced (pyrrhotite field), at about 400 to 550 °C. Oxidised magmas keep sulphur as sulphate, not sulphide. So Cu and Au are not trapped in early sulphide minerals. They stay in the melt, and fluid can later carry them away. slides 19 and 49 (Hedenquist and Richards, 1998) lecture recording, 9 Oct

The rocks 39 to 52 m

The host intrusions

Porphyry host rocks are intermediate to felsic I-type intrusions: granodiorite, tonalite, quartz monzodiorite and diorite. They are not true granites. I-type means they came from igneous source rock, not from melted sediment. In island arcs, they come from the upper mantle or recycled oceanic crust. In continental arcs, they show crustal contamination. There are usually several intrusions, and the one that formed the ore is usually late in the series. slide 22 lecture recording, 9 Oct

Compare the grain size in A and B. Which texture gives porphyry deposits their name? What does it tell you about how the magma cooled?

Show what I see
  • A: evenly coarse grained, with all crystals about the same size: pink K-feldspar, white plagioclase and quartz, and black biotite or hornblende. An equigranular granitoid such as granodiorite.
  • B: large, well-formed feldspar crystals (phenocrysts) in a much finer groundmass.

So B shows porphyritic texture, which gives the deposits their name. It shows two cooling rates. Crystals grew slowly at depth. Then the magma rose to a shallow level, and the rest crystallised quickly. Porphyry deposits form at exactly this shallow, high level.

Deposit scale 52 to 65 m

The Lowell and Guilbert shells

An orebody is shaped like an upside-down cup or a cylinder, 0.5 to 2 km tall and 0.1 to 2 km across. It is a large permeable zone of fractured rock, often near the edge of the intrusion and along a major fault. The ore occurs as disseminated grains; as a of irregular quartz-sulphide veinlets (the main ore in most deposits); as larger straight veins; and in breccias. slide 24

The classic model of Lowell and Guilbert divides the deposit into shells. In the middle is a barren or low-grade core, with magnetite and little sulphide. Around it is the ore shell: 1 to 3% sulphide, with more chalcopyrite than pyrite, and molybdenite. Outside that is the pyrite shell, with about 10% pyrite and almost no chalcopyrite. Furthest out are a few peripheral veins with Pb, Zn, Ag and Au. So you do not mine the centre of the porphyry. The ore is around its edges. slides 24 and 50 lecture recording, 9 Oct

Name the vein0 / 4

The system came from one deposit, and B veins are not found everywhere. So many academics prefer the system of Seedorff and others (2005). It classifies veins by their minerals and their alteration halo. From oldest to youngest: potassic veins (magnetite, biotite, then sugary quartz A veins); sodic or calcic veins; quartz veins with no halo (including banded quartz-molybdenite veins); veins with strong hydrolytic (sericitic or greisen) halos; and finally late, low-temperature or distal veins. slides 31 and 32

At the lab test
  • The lab ore suites include Bingham Canyon, Ok Tedi and Goonumbla (Endeavour) in NSW. Bingham has good B veins with molybdenite, and EB (biotite) veins.
  • A vein: sugary quartz with a K-feldspar halo. B vein: planar quartz with a centre line, no halo, coarse molybdenite and chalcopyrite. D vein: pyrite-rich with a bleached sericite halo.
  • Primary ore minerals: pyrite, chalcopyrite, bornite, enargite, magnetite, molybdenite; supergene chalcocite and covellite; oxide malachite and cuprite.

Field task · potassic alterationslide 52

In A and B, what colour is the K-feldspar? What dark mineral is growing in B? Why does this colour suggest an oxidised magma?

Show what I see
  • A: K-feldspar (Kfs) alteration along the edges of quartz veins, at Bethsaida, British Columbia.
  • B: widespread pink K-feldspar with patches of fine hydrothermal biotite (Bt), and quartz-molybdenite veinlets, at Red Mountain, Yukon.

This is potassic alteration, which is always present in the core. The K-feldspar is strongly pink because it contains iron, which is a sign of an oxidised system. In many porphyries you will not see much K-feldspar. Secondary biotite is the more common sign of potassic alteration. slide 52 lecture recording, 9 Oct

Field task · the outer haloslide 53

Look at f, the yellow-green radiating mineral. What is it? Which alteration zone does it show you are in?

Show what I see
  • The cores and outcrop are green-grey: chlorite.
  • f: a coarse, radiating, pistachio-green mass that forms a nodule in an altered volcanic clast: epidote.

So you are in the propylitic zone: chlorite, epidote, albite and calcite, with magnetite or pyrite. It is always present and forms the outer halo. The first epidote marks the start of the propylitic zone. These samples are from Schaft Creek, BC. slide 53 (Scott et al., 2008)

Phyllic and argillic alteration are important for grade, not only for finding the direction to ore. The sericite and chlorite-sericite fluids that come late can dissolve copper again and remove it, because copper becomes mobile in these acidic conditions. slide 9 lecture recording, 9 Oct

Exploration 91 to 104 m

UST textures, Sr/Y and magnetics

(USTs) are banded, comb-like layers in the cupola, which is the dome-shaped top of an intrusion. Crystals of quartz, feldspar, mica, amphibole, tourmaline and apatite grow downwards into the intrusion, all in the same direction. They are often coarse or pegmatitic. They act as chemical and physical barriers to circulating fluid. So finding them helps you find the ore: porphyry Cu-Au ore can be 100 to 200 m above the UST zone. They form in open systems (fluid is released by fracturing after each layer) or in closed systems (fluid is trapped as they crystallise). Not every porphyry has them. slides 33 to 35 (Müller et al., 2023)

Field task · which way is up?slide 35

In e (Umnu Hutul), the grey quartz crystals grow from one side of each band. Which way was the top of the intrusion?

Show what I see
  • e: repeated pale bands. Each band has a flat side, and grey quartz combs with points that all face the same way.
  • a, b, d: wavy to contorted banding. b is altered to tourmaline.

USTs grow downwards into the magma from the roof. So the flat side that each layer grows from faces up, towards the cupola, and the crystal points face down. This is easy to read in the straight and wavy types, but much harder in the contorted or brecciated types. slides 33 to 35 lecture recording, 9 Oct

Geochemistry also helps, and it is cheap, using XRF. Fertile porphyry magmas (magmas that can form ore) contain a lot of water, rich in H₂O, SO₃ and Cl. Extra water stops plagioclase from crystallising, and plagioclase takes in Sr. Instead, hornblende crystallises, and it takes in Y. So fertile magmas have a high Sr/Y ratio at a given SiO₂, compared with barren arc rocks. slide 36 (Rohrlach and Loucks, 2005)

You want a porphyry that started rich in magnetite and was then flushed by a lot of fluid, because the fluid brought the metal. Mechanism 104 to 117 m

Carapace, hydrofracturing and fluids

A porphyry magma starts at about 1000 °C with 3 to 5% water. As it crystallises, the water cannot stay in the crystals. So it collects in the last melt at the top and edges of the intrusion, which becomes saturated in water. This is the . Pressure builds until the brittle rock above breaks. Hydrofracturing forms the stockwork, and breccia pipes and dykes push upwards. Go through it step by step. slides 46 to 48 lecture recording, 9 Oct

The fluids are salty brines rich in metals, between about 250 and 750 °C. They are very different mixtures of magmatic and meteoric water. Sulphur isotopes in pyrite and chalcopyrite are often magmatic, while later sulphate is often meteoric. The evidence that the metals are magmatic is indirect. Meteoric water is pulled in around the stock and mixes with magmatic fluid. This drives the widespread sericitic alteration with disseminated sulphide. Above the system, there can be acid-sulphate alteration and alkaline hot springs. slides 43 and 45 lecture recording, 9 Oct

Variation 117 to 130 m

Porphyry Mo and Climax

Porphyry Mo deposits have most of the same features as Cu-Au porphyries. But they come from magmas that are more reduced and more fractionated. There are three types, based on the host rock: granitic (biotite granite and granodiorite, poor in Cu, like Endako in BC); trondhjemite (unusual, not very rich in silica, like Trout Lake in BC); and rhyolitic porphyry (rhyolite dykes and sills that formed just below the surface, like Climax in Colorado). slide 38

Field task · Climax oreslide 40

This is ore. What would you look for to see the molybdenum?

Show what I see
  • A pale rock rich in quartz (silicified rhyolite porphyry), with dark grey streaks and a thin quartz vein.

Look for soft, lead-grey to bluish, platy molybdenite in the veinlets and scattered through the rock. Most ore is in stockwork veins, with a little disseminated ore, at only 0.1 to 0.45% MoS₂. So you will not see much. Deposits range from 50 to 1500 Mt. They are mined by open cut, stoping and block caving, mostly in western North America (and now Tibet). slide 40 lecture recording, 9 Oct

A plot of oxidation (Fe₂O₃/FeO) against fractionation (Rb/Sr) separates the porphyry types. Cu-Au comes from oxidised magmas with little fractionation. Mo comes from more fractionated magmas. Sn-W comes from reduced, highly fractionated magmas. Phil Blevin used this plot for New South Wales. Every NSW porphyry plots as oxidised and only a little fractionated. The big picture 130 to 144 m

Why the Andes

Stress controls how magma behaves. Tension lets primitive magma rise quickly up extensional faults and erupt. Compression stops it rising, which builds a large MASH zone and a large reservoir in the lower crust. Shear is ideal: local extensional faults and pull-apart basins focus magma and fluid into stocks at a high level. slide 49 (Richards, 2004)

Which stress regime?0 / 3

The largest porphyries form in contractional settings of three kinds: (1) flat-slab subduction caused by fast plate movement; (2) subduction of young, buoyant seamounts, ridges or oceanic plateaus, which also makes the slab flatter; and (3) accretion of exotic terranes. The central Andes are the largest copper region in the world (985 Mt of contained Cu), for four reasons. They are in the middle of the orogen, where shortening and oroclinal bending are greatest. The Juan Fernandez ridge was subducted there. Copper-forming events happened again and again from the Late Carboniferous to the Pliocene, which suggests that the crust was rich in copper from the start. And they are young (Cenozoic), so they have not yet been eroded. slides 54 to 57

Preservation is the problem. Porphyries form high in rising mountain belts, so they are eroded quickly. Palaeozoic porphyries are uncommon around the world, which makes the Lachlan Orogen porphyries of central NSW unusual. A few Proterozoic deposits have also been interpreted as porphyries. slide 37 lecture recording, 9 Oct

Here is the whole story. Fluids from the slab add water to the mantle wedge and oxidise it, and add S, Cl and C. Basalt with water rises and changes the lower crust. Copper comes mainly from the subducted oceanic crust, which contains more copper than continental crust (as well as subducted VMS deposits, manganese nodules and deep-sea sediment). Very deep faults that cut the whole lithosphere focus the magma upwards. Flat subduction can enrich the lower crust and lithospheric mantle, which melt later. New mafic melt injected into chambers in the middle crust makes the mixed magma that forms the deposit. slides 58 to 60

Memorise
  • Oxidised, hydrous, I-type intermediate magma from a MASH zone; shear (pull-apart) is the ideal stress regime.
  • Giants form in contractional settings: flat slab, ridge or seamount subduction, terrane accretion.
  • Central Andes: maximum shortening, Juan Fernandez ridge, repeated episodes, young and preserved.
