Reading the core

Drilling is the only way to see what is really below the surface, and the only way to prove that an ore body is there. This lab covers how the three main types of drilling work, what each one costs and what it gives you, how a core tray is marked, how to measure a structure in oriented core, and how to log what you see. Why drill?

Mapping, geochemistry and geophysics tell you where to look. Only drilling lets you see the real geology below the surface and sample it directly. It is the only way to work out the possible size, shape and style of an ore deposit, and the only way to know if you have one at all. You also have to drill and assay to show that an ore body could be profitable. In Australia, the JORC Code requires this before you can report a resource. slide 4

Concept 1 12 to 24 m

RAB, RC and diamond

Mineral exploration uses three main drilling methods, which are very different from oil and gas drilling. (RAB) is the cheap, fast first method, most common in exploration. A drill head made of polycrystalline diamond composite breaks the rock as it goes down. Compressed air blows the broken pieces (cuttings) up the outside of the rods, and they are put on the ground, one pile for every metre. slides 5 to 9

Field task · rows of pilesslide 7

What is each small pile? What does the change in colour along a row tell you? How much would you trust these samples?

Show what I see
  • Long rows of small piles of rock chips in front of the rig.
  • Along each row, the colour changes from red-brown to orange to pale cream.

Each pile is the cuttings from one metre of a RAB hole, and each row is one hole, read from the top down. The colour change shows the hole passing down through the weathering profile. RAB is useful for a first look, but the samples are low quality. The cuttings come up outside the rods, so chips from higher in the hole can mix in. slides 7, 9 and 27

(RC) is the main method in exploration, and for drilling known targets such as mapped gossans or geochemical anomalies. The rods have two tubes. Water goes down the outer tube, and the cuttings come back up the inner tube. So the cuttings are much less contaminated than RAB chips. slides 10 to 16

(DD) usually comes after good RC assays. A ring-shaped diamond bit cuts a solid cylinder of rock. The cylinder slides up into an inner tube, which is pulled out on a wire. You get solid core from a known depth, with no contamination, so you can see the geology directly. You need this to prove a deposit and report it to the stock exchange. After logging and photography, the core is usually cut in half. One half is crushed and assayed, and the other half is kept for reference. Gold deposits are the exception: because of the , all of the core is often assayed. slides 17 to 24

The costs include everything: the drilling company, the site geologist, transport to the core shed, and logging and sampling per metre. Many holes are at least 300 m long now, so one 300 m diamond hole at $400 per metre costs $120 000. (On slide 27, the "technique" cell for diamond drilling repeats the air-blast text of the other two methods. In fact, diamond drilling cuts core, as on slides 17 to 23.) slides 27 and 28

There are two more decisions. Hole angle: RAB and RC holes are usually vertical. Most diamond holes are drilled at 60° to cover the most ground, although resource drilling is often vertical. Whatever the method, choose an angle that cuts across the local structures and the expected shape of the ore body. Sterilisation drilling: after the resource is defined, but before you decide where to put the processing plant, waste dumps, tailings dam and offices, you drill those sites. This makes sure that you do not build on top of ore. Many companies do not do enough of this, because it costs money and has no obvious benefit. slides 29 and 30

Which drilling?0 / 6

Memorise
  • RAB: cheap (~$150/m), fast, low quality, to ~125 m, greenfields.
  • RC: ~$200/m, reliable chips, to ~500 m, brownfields and known targets.
  • Diamond: ~$400/m, core from a known depth, to ~1800 m; needed to prove and report a deposit; half core assayed, all of it for gold.
  • Sterilisation drilling checks infrastructure sites for ore before you build.

Oriented core: alpha and beta

A loose cylinder of rock can turn around. So by itself, it cannot tell you the strike and dip of any bedding, vein or contact. You need an orientation mark. This costs extra and takes time, because it needs orientation equipment in the hole (digital now), so it is not always done. When it is done, the drillers draw an along the core with a wax pencil: a straight line with arrows pointing down the hole. slides 41 to 43

Field task · red linesslide 43

What is the long red line? What are the short angled marks for?

Show what I see
  • A straight red line along the length of the core.
  • Short red marks at an angle to it, like arrowheads, all pointing in the same direction.

It is the orientation line, and the marks are arrows pointing down the hole. You measure every structure on this core from that line. So you always know which way was down and which side was the bottom. slides 41 to 43

Two angles describe a planar structure in oriented core. is the angle between the core axis and the long axis of the ellipse that the plane makes where it cuts the core. 90° means the plane is at right angles to the core, and close to 0° means it runs along the core. is measured around the core, clockwise when looking down the hole, from the bottom-of-hole line to the lower end of the long axis of the ellipse. Together with the orientation of the hole itself, these two angles give you the true strike and dip. To measure a structure, it must cut completely through the core. slides 44 to 47

At the bench 72 to 84 m

What to log

Every company has its own logging method and its own codes and symbols. Most now log directly into spreadsheets with short codes on a strong laptop. The content is the same everywhere: lithology; grain size (average and range); fracturing (how strong and how far apart); mineralisation (type and intensity); alteration (type and intensity); structures (type and extent); and core loss. slides 51 and 52

Two habits are more important than the codes. First, always check the metre marks, because field assistants usually write them and mistakes are quite common. Second, always log with a hand lens, a steel scratcher (for hardness) and dilute hydrochloric acid next to you. slide 51

At the lab test

  • For any vein or contact, say its angle to the core axis, and use the hand lens, scratcher and acid before you name a mineral.

Both are from the weathered zone. What has happened to A? What does the slide call the depth in B?

Show what I see
  • A: soft-looking, pale pink to orange, cracked all over, with no fresh minerals visible.
  • B: broken, crumbly blocks with yellow-brown iron staining and some grey rock.

A is completely weathered rock: kaolinite and hematite from weathering, the clay and iron oxide at the top of the profile. B is the base of oxidation at 23 m: below this depth the sulphides are fresh. Log it carefully, because oxide ore and primary ore need different processing. slides 55 and 56

Field task · two broken zonesslides 57 and 58
A · 40.4 to 40.6 m
B · a whole row of broken core

A is a crushed zone 20 cm wide. B is broken over a long length of core. What are they? Why is B so long?

Show what I see
  • A: a narrow band of crushed, granular material, finer than the rock on each side.
  • B: broken pieces along most of a row in the tray, with solid core above and below.

A is a shear zone with gouge, which is rock ground up along a fault. B is a fault zone that is almost parallel to the core axis. The hole went down along the fault instead of across it. So you get metres of broken core and almost no information about the orientation of the fault. This is why you angle holes across the expected structures. slides 30, 57 and 58

Field task · 221 mslide 59

Log the host rock and the veins. What happened to the veins after they formed?

Show what I see
  • Dark grey, fine-grained host rock.
  • White veins bent into tight loops and hooks.

These are folded quartz veins in siltstone. The veins formed first. Then the rock was deformed and they folded with it. So the veins are older than the deformation, or formed early in it. This relative timing is important for orogenic gold, where you want to know which generation of veins contains the gold. slide 59

Field task · 802.6 mslide 60

The thin pale vein is gypsum. Estimate its alpha angle. Is it a good structure to measure?

Show what I see
  • A thin pale vein that crosses the core almost straight across.
  • A red line along the core: the orientation (or cut) line.

The vein is at about 80° to the core axis, almost at right angles. It cuts completely through the core, so you can measure it: alpha is 80°, and with the orientation line you could also measure beta. slides 47 and 60

Field task · 375.9 mslide 61

The core is massive sulphide. What do you think the blue circles mark? Why draw them?

Show what I see
  • Fine-grained, grey, metallic rock with almost no gangue.
  • Small blue circles drawn around tiny bright specks.

It is massive sulphide with visible gold, and each grain is circled in blue. Visible gold is logged and marked so that nobody misses it, and the interval is sampled carefully. Because of the nugget effect, gold core is often assayed whole instead of cut in half. slides 17 and 61

When core goes bad

All sulphides are unstable at the Earth's surface. They react with oxygen and change to oxides. So core keeps changing after it is drilled. Core with disseminated sulphides is especially at risk. Core stored in a humid or tropical place (warmer and wetter) breaks down much faster than core in a dry climate. Sulphide oxidation can even destroy metal core trays. The only solutions are to wrap the core in plastic film (cling wrap) or to store it in a freezer. slides 62 and 64

Both are tuff-breccia from the Bowdens silver deposit. One was drilled in 2021, and the other in 2025. Which is which? What happened to the older one?

Show what I see

A is the older core, drilled in 2021 (164.7 m), and B is the 2025 core (102.5 m). The sulphides in A oxidised in storage, which stained the core and broke it up. A few years in a core yard was enough. Log and photograph core as soon as it comes out of the ground. slides 62 and 63
