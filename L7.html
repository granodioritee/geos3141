How nickel ends up in sulphide ore

In a mafic or ultramafic magma, each nickel atom can go to one of two places. It can enter the crystal structure of olivine, the first mineral to grow, and stay spread through the rock forever. Or it can dissolve in droplets of sulphide liquid, which sink and collect to form ore. Magmatic nickel deposits only form where something made the nickel go into the sulphide. This lecture explains how this happens. It also covers the deposits in the Western Australian goldfields, where 2.7 billion year old lava formed nickel ore in this way.

Straight from the mantle

Magmatic ore deposits form inside the magma itself, while it crystallises. They do not form from fluids that leave the magma later. The main groups are: nickel-copper sulphide deposits (Archaean deposits hosted in komatiite, deposits in conduits of mafic intrusions, and layered mafic-ultramafic complexes); primary platinum group element (PGE) deposits; chromium-vanadium-titanium oxide deposits (layered complexes and podiform chromitites); phosphorus deposits in syenites and carbonatites; rare earth deposits; and diamonds in kimberlites and lamproites. All of them come from primary magmas that formed in the mantle. slide 4

The examples in this lecture come from many years of field work: PGEs in podiform chromitites at Mount Lightning in southern NSW, the chromitites of the Tumut Serpentinite Province, the Bushveld Complex in South Africa, and nickel in Laos. Podiform chromitites are important because they supply metallurgical-grade chromium, which is used in chrome steel. slide 3 lecture recording, 28 Sep

This lecture covers the petrology you need, and the nickel-copper sulphide deposits hosted in komatiites. Lecture 8 covers the mafic intrusions, and the PGE, oxide, phosphorus and nickel laterite deposits.

Refresher 12 to 26 m

Petrology review: important names

There are two kinds of rock names, and you should keep them separate. Acid, intermediate, basic and ultrabasic are chemical terms, based on the amount of silica. Felsic, mafic and ultramafic are mineral terms, based on the minerals in the rock. They usually match, but they mean different things. slide 6

Chemical nameSiO₂MineralogyExample
Acid (felsic)over 63%mostly feldspars and quartzgranite
Intermediate55 to 63%mostly feldspars, some ferromagnesian mineralsandesite
Basic (mafic)45 to 55%mostly dark minerals plus plagioclasebasalt, gabbro
Ultrabasic (ultramafic)under 45%olivine and pyroxene, or hornblendedunite, peridotite

Magmatic ore forms in ultramafic and mafic rocks. Among the gabbroic rocks, remember norite, a pyroxene-rich gabbro where the pyroxene is orthopyroxene. Norite hosts much of the world's PGE and some of its sulphide ore. Ultramafic rocks are named by their amounts of olivine, orthopyroxene and clinopyroxene. (olivine plus orthopyroxene) makes up most of the oceanic mantle. (olivine with both pyroxenes) makes up most of the continental mantle. Wehrlite and websterite are much rarer. slides 6 and 7 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Concept 1 26 to 40 m

The olivine problem

A basic or ultrabasic magma can separate into two liquids that do not mix, like oil and water. One is a silicate melt, and the other is a much denser . This sulphide liquid separates at 1200 to 1400 °C, at the top of Bowen's reaction series. Nickel, copper and the PGEs prefer to go into the sulphide liquid. slide 8

The problem is olivine. In Bowen's series, olivine crystallises first from a basic magma, and nickel easily replaces magnesium in its structure. If olivine grows early, it takes up the nickel, and no nickel is left for the sulphide. So the key point on the slide is olivine suppression. To make a nickel sulphide deposit, the magma must not crystallise olivine early. It must crystallise pyroxene first. Three things can cause this, and all are types of contamination: slides 7 and 8 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Add silicaOlivine plus silica makes pyroxene. A magma that takes in silica-rich crust crystallises pyroxene immediately.
Add ironContamination by iron-rich rock makes the magma crystallise iron-rich pyroxene first.
Reduce itA reducing environment, such as a magma that intrudes through a coal bed, has the same effect.

What if olivine is not suppressed? Then the nickel stays spread through the olivine. This rock can still become ore much later. Weathering can break down the olivine and concentrate the nickel in a laterite. lecture recording, 28 Sep

Cool a basic magma1400 °C

Order of crystallisation from slide 8 (1200 to 1400 °C at the top, 700 to 800 °C at the bottom). The spacing between minerals is not to scale.

Memorise
  • Sulphide melt is immiscible with silicate melt and separates at 1200 to 1400 °C; Ni, Cu and PGE go into it.
  • Olivine crystallising early steals the nickel. Suppress olivine (silica, iron or reducing contamination) and nickel goes into the sulphide.
Concept 2 40 to 52 m

Settling: segregation and cumulates

When dense minerals or droplets form, gravity concentrates them. Heavy minerals crystallise, sink to the floor of the magma chamber, and collect as a layer or band. This is called segregation. It can concentrate chromite, magnetite and ilmenite, and also sulphide droplets that carry Cu, Ni, Pt and the other PGEs. slides 9 and 10

Plagioclase has about the same density as a mafic magma, so it does not sink. It usually floats to the top of the chamber, as you can see in the Bushveld and other layered intrusions. The spinels (chromite, magnetite), the sulphides and the platinum group minerals sink easily. slide 9 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Let the chamber settleready

Each particle sinks faster if it is much denser than the magma (density 2.8).

The classic example is the Merensky Reef in the Bushveld gabbro-norite of South Africa. It is a layer only 1 to 5 m thick, rich in Pt, Fe, Ni, Cu and Cr. A layer this thin could probably not be mined anywhere else. It was mined because there were many workers available there. slide 10 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Concept 3 52 to 66 m

Where the large deposits are

There are two main kinds of primary Ni-Cu sulphide deposit. Archaean deposits hosted in komatiite are mostly in the Norseman-Wiluna greenstone belt of Western Australia and the Abitibi greenstone belt of Canada. Deposits related to mafic intrusions include Voisey's Bay (Canada), Jinchuan (China; many pipe-shaped bodies instead of one intrusion, and most of China's nickel), Pechenga and Norilsk (Russia), Sudbury (Canada), Kabanga (Tanzania), the Duluth Complex (Minnesota) and the Giles Complex in the Musgrave Ranges, on the border of South Australia and Western Australia. slide 11 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Grade and tonnage work against each other. The highest-grade deposits are usually small, and the largest deposits are usually low grade. Victor and Cosmos in WA have more than 10% Ni, but only about a million tonnes. Duluth is the largest of all, but its grade is very low. Norilsk is the exception: it is large and rich. slides 14 and 15

One unusual deposit is Avebury in Tasmania, a remobilised nickel deposit. Devonian granites intruded ultramafic rocks. Convection driven by the heat removed nickel from the ultramafic silicate minerals and concentrated it at the granite contact. The deposit was high grade but small, in very difficult ground, and it closed. lecture recording, 28 Sep

Economics 66 to 76 m

How laterites took over

In the past, most of the world's nickel came from primary sulphides, mainly at Norilsk and Sudbury. Now laterites supply about 65%, and most production comes from Indonesia, the Philippines and New Caledonia. The laterites were known in the 1950s, but they were too expensive to process. New processing methods changed this. Indonesia's growth was paid for mainly by Chinese investment in processing plants on Sulawesi. These plants process ore from many small deposits in one central plant. slides 16 and 17 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Main nickel producers (t Ni)2023

Slide 17, source USGS 2026.

Australian production fell from 160 000 to 45 000 t, because Australia cannot compete on cost. Ten years ago, only Canada produced more. Mount Keith is still the largest Australian mine, and most of Kambalda is closed for now. Platinum is a valuable by-product: even at about 0.6 g/t, it adds useful value to WA nickel ore. South Africa produces most of the world's platinum, from the Merensky Reef. slide 16 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Concept 4 76 to 88 m

What the ore is made of

In these deposits you see pyrrhotite almost everywhere, because it makes up most of the sulphide. It causes problems. It oxidises very quickly, and the reaction releases heat. In some WA nickel mines, the waste piles have caught fire by themselves. The mineral you want is pentlandite, by far the main nickel mineral. It occurs as small grains or along fractures in the pyrrhotite. Chalcopyrite is the only common copper mineral. Minor minerals are millerite (NiS, in the supergene zone), the PGE minerals cooperite ((Pt,Pd,Ni)S) and sperrylite (PtAs₂), and magnetite. The gangue is olivine, pyroxene and plagioclase. Grades range from 0.2% to 12% Ni. slide 18 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Concept 5 88 to 102 m

Komatiites: lava that flowed like water

The komatiite-hosted deposits formed in the Archaean, 2.7 to 3.0 billion years ago, when the mantle was much hotter. are ultramafic lavas made almost only of olivine and pyroxene. They erupted from fissures on the seafloor at about 1400 °C (modern Hawaiian basalts erupt at about 1000 to 1200 °C). They had very low viscosity and flowed like water. They formed thick channels that cooled slowly, with cumulate textures, and thin sheet flows that cooled very quickly against the cold seawater, with . Between the flows there were sediments with sulphide. These sediments supplied the sulphur. slide 20 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Field task · a komatiite flow topslide 1

Name the texture. What does it tell you about how this lava cooled?

Show what I see

This is spinifex texture: long, thin, blade-shaped crystals (originally olivine) that cross each other, like the spinifex grass of the WA outback. It forms when ultramafic lava cools very fast, in the thin sheet flows and at the tops of flows. The rock is green because metamorphism changed the olivine to serpentine. This is why the host belts are called greenstone belts.

The Norseman-Wiluna belt runs north from Norseman to Wiluna. It has magmatic nickel sulphide deposits and orogenic gold along its whole length. The nickel is syngenetic (it formed with the lavas). The gold is epigenetic and is in later structures. Later deformation, metamorphism and granite intrusion have hidden much of the original igneous character. slides 20 to 22 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Case study 102 to 116 m

Kambalda: lava rivers

Kambalda is near Kalgoorlie, next to the salt lake of Lake Lefroy. Canadian explorers found nothing there in the 1950s and 1960s. They explored in the Canadian way, on fresh rock exposed by glaciers. But at Kambalda there is about 100 m of weathered material on top. Western Mining, a small Australian company, understood weathered terrain. It found the nickel quickly using soil geochemistry. lecture recording, 28 Sep

A is Lake Lefroy, a salt lake. One mine goes under it. B shows the Kambalda open cuts in the 1970s. What problem does the salt lake cause when you mine sulphide ore under it?

Show the reasoning

Salt water and oxidising sulphides damage equipment. In that mine, equipment lasts no more than about four years, because everything rusts and the oxidising sulphides make sulphuric acid. This makes mining under the lake very expensive. Open cuts (B) were a cheap way to start when the ore was close to the surface.

The Kambalda Dome is probably an old magma chamber that was later folded. The nickel ore shoots (Hunt, Red Hill, Lunnon, Victor, Fisher, Durkin and others) are at the base of the komatiite sequence, on the contact with the footwall basalt. Here, sediments with sulphide between the flows supplied the sulphur. slides 23 and 27 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Think of the lava as a river. Komatiite at 1400 °C, flowing like water, melted and eroded the floor it flowed over (thermal erosion). It cut channels into the basalt and melted the sediments with sulphide into the flow. Their sulphur combined with nickel from the lava, and a sulphide liquid separated. What happened next depended on how fast the lava was flowing. slide 24 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Concept 6 116 to 130 m

Type 1: the Kambalda ore shoots

Kambalda-style (Type 1) ore is in lava channels, in both flood flow and compound flow facies, usually in the lowest flow, where thermal erosion cut into the rocks below. The ore bodies are long ribbons: 1 to 5 m thick, 50 to 100 m wide and 100 to 700 m long. Groups of them reach up to 2 km down plunge. Most are less than 5 Mt, and many are less than 1 Mt. They are layered from the base upwards: slide 32

Massive sulphideMore than 80% sulphide, an irregular zone at the base about 2 m thick, 2 to 20% Ni.
Matrix sulphideA more regular zone where sulphide fills the spaces between silicate grains, 1 to 6% Ni.
Disseminated sulphideLess than 40% sulphide, an irregular upper zone, 1 to 2% Ni.

Later, the geology moved them. Folding, faulting and later dykes and stocks have cut and moved the shoots. So today the ore is not where it formed, and this is why drilling is so important. Metamorphism grew coarse pyrite and secondary magnetite in the matrix ore. The magnetite is useful, because magnetic surveys can find the ore. Faulting also made the host rock weaker. It changed ultramafic rock to serpentinite, with talc and magnesite alteration where fluid flowed through. slides 31 and 32 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Concept 7 130 to 140 m

Type 2: Mount Keith

Mount Keith-style (Type 2) ore formed in lava channels with lower contacts that cut across the layers below. It formed as cumulate flow units within flood flow facies. The orebody is a cumulate unit that is thicker in one area: up to 800 m thick, 1 to 3 km wide, and more than 1 km down plunge. The sulphide is disseminated, only 1 to 5% of the rock, as grains in an olivine cumulate that is now serpentinite. The grade is less than 1% Ni (about 0.6%). Pentlandite is the main mineral, and copper sulphides occur only in the lower part. Metamorphism and fluids have changed it, especially along structures. It also has a long history of weathering: when Mount Keith opened, laterite ore was mined first, before the sulphide. slide 33 lecture recording, 28 Sep

Why mine 0.6% nickel? Because of size and access: Mount Keith is about a billion tonnes (1022 Mt on slide 15), and it could be mined as an open cut from the surface. slide 15 lecture recording, 28 Sep
