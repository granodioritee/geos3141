Tin, tungsten and the last fluids of a granite

Every phone and car you have used contains tin solder, and tungsten makes many things hard, from drill bits to armour. Both come mostly from granites. These are highly fractionated granites, rich in volatiles. Their last hot fluids concentrate tin, tungsten, bismuth and molybdenum in veins and greisens. These deposits also have many beautiful mineral specimens, so this page is also practice for hand specimens. First, watch how one granite zones its metals as its fluid cools.

Why these metals matter

This lecture is about magmatic-hydrothermal deposits related to granites: tin (Sn), tungsten (W), bismuth (Bi) and molybdenum (Mo). Tin is the one that we cannot live without. Its main use is solder, about half of all tin mined. After hundreds of years, there is still no better replacement. You cannot build a computer, phone or car without it. This is why the USA and many other countries list tin as a critical mineral, and the USA produces no tin. One concern: about 40% of mined tin comes from small-scale (artisanal) miners or small companies, especially in Brazil, Indonesia and Myanmar. slides 2 to 4 and 11 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Who mines it, who holds it

USGS figures from slides 2, 3, 12 and 18 (tin in kt; tungsten and bismuth in tonnes). The slide marks Australia's tungsten reserve with an asterisk.

In Australia, the largest tin producer by far is Renison Bell on the west coast of Tasmania, with reserves of at least 180 kt Sn. The largest proven tin deposit in NSW is Taronga in the New England Fold Belt: 22 Mt at 0.16% Sn, about 36 kt of tin metal. This deposit has opened and closed as the tin price has changed. World tin reserves have been falling, from about 6100 kt in 2010 to 4700 kt in 2019. slides 3 and 4 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Mineralogy 13 to 26 m

Tin minerals: cassiterite and stannite

Almost all tin ever mined comes from two minerals. , SnO₂, is tetragonal, with an adamantine lustre. Its hardness is 6 to 7 (between feldspar and quartz), and it is very dense (SG about 7.0). Its colour changes with impurities: black, brown, yellow, white or red. It forms short crystals with similar dimensions in all directions, or short prisms ("needle tin"). It also forms rounded, fibrous "wood tin" and granular masses. slide 5 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Field task · wood tinslide 7

This does not look like the tin ore you would expect. Describe the banding, and suggest how it formed.

Show what I see
  • Round, wavy bands that look like wood grain, cream and yellow-brown to dark brown, with a fibrous texture.

This is wood tin, a fibrous, colloform cassiterite from Cornwall. The bands record minerals deposited one after another from a hydrothermal vein: quartz, then cassiterite, then quartz, then cassiterite, again and again. It looks nothing like normal cassiterite, and this is why you should remember it. slide 7 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Some cassiterite is unusual. At Wheal Coates in Cornwall, cassiterite has replaced orthoclase crystals and kept their shape. These pseudomorphs are found nowhere else on Earth. At Emmaville in north-eastern NSW, it has replaced fossil gastropods (snail shells). Gem-quality cassiterite cut like a diamond has the same adamantine lustre, but is much denser. Most cassiterite mined in eastern Australia has been alluvial: it was washed out of the granites into river gravels. But there is still a lot of potential for hard-rock tin. slides 8 and 9 lecture recording, 26 Oct

  • Stannite Cu₂FeSnS₄: grey, metallic, in reduced, sulphide-rich systems.

Mineralogy 26 to 39 m

Tungsten: scheelite and wolframite

Only two tungsten minerals are important. , CaWO₄, is colourless, white, golden yellow, brown or orange, with a vitreous to adamantine lustre. It is quite soft (H 4.5 to 5) and dense (SG 6.1), and is usually massive or granular, sometimes in shapes like octahedra. The best way to recognise it is its bright blue-white fluorescence under ultraviolet light. So explorers really do look for scheelite at night with UV lamps. slides 13 and 14 lecture recording, 26 Oct

About 65% of tungsten is used to make tungsten carbide, which is valued for its hardness. The rest is used in steels and superalloys (17%), tungsten metal (10%) and chemicals (8%). Tungsten has the highest melting point (3422 °C) and boiling point (5930 °C) of any element, a density of 19.25, and high resistance to corrosion. Armour plating and hardened ammunition are important military uses. China produces most of the world's tungsten, so the supply of tungsten is a strategic concern. Australia produces little tungsten, but has large reserves. slides 12 and 17 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Mineralogy 39 to 52 m

Bismuth and molybdenum

Bismuth occurs in many minerals, but the economic ones are bismuthinite (Bi₂S₃: lead grey, metallic, soft, H 2 to 2.5, SG 6.78, prismatic to needle-shaped, often fibrous) and native bismuth (cream-white to reddish-white, metallic, soft, SG about 9.7 to 9.8, mostly massive with strong cleavage). China produces the most, followed, surprisingly, by Laos. Bismuth is used in fire detectors, fire extinguishers and electrical fuses, and, as bismuth subsalicylate, in medicine for stomach problems. It also has an unusual physical property: solid bismuth is less dense than liquid bismuth. slides 18 to 21 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Bismuthinite, slide 20
Native bismuth, slide 20

Molybdenum has one ore mineral, molybdenite (MoS₂: lead grey to black, metallic, very soft at H 1 to 1.5, SG about 4.6 to 4.7, in flexible, platy hexagonal crystals). At the surface, where there is iron and water, it changes to bright yellow, powdery ferrimolybdite, or to pale blue ilsemannite. Molybdenum is mostly added to steel to make it stronger, harder, more conductive, and more resistant to wear and corrosion. It is also used in lubricants, pigments and catalysts. slides 22 to 24 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Mineralogy 52 to 65 m

The gangue

The ore minerals are simple, but the gangue is not. Sn-W-Mo deposits related to granites contain quartz (often smoky), alkali feldspars (orthoclase, microcline), plagioclase (mostly albite, because these are lower-temperature systems), micas (muscovite, biotite and lithium micas), fluorite, apatite, beryl, black schorl tourmaline, topaz, carbonates (siderite, calcite) and sulphides (pyrite, pyrrhotite, sphalerite, chalcopyrite, arsenopyrite). The lithium micas can be a resource themselves. Greenbushes in WA, now the world's largest hard-rock lithium mine, was first mined for tin (see Lecture 18). slide 25 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Concept 1 78 to 91 m

The granites behind them

This lecture focuses on vein and greisen Sn-W-Mo deposits. They are linked to felsic granites that intruded at shallow depths, usually 1.5 to 5 km. The granites are rich in K (a lot of K-feldspar and micas), metaluminous to peraluminous, and highly fractionated (high Rb/Sr). There are two series. The is linked to Mo-W, and the is linked to Sn-W. The fluids come from the intrusions themselves. But convection may also dissolve metals, and especially sulphur, from the country rocks. slide 28 lecture recording, 26 Oct

They form during uplift after mountain building (back arcs and the interiors of continents), and in crust made thicker by collision between continents. Most are in Phanerozoic orogenic belts. They form belts parallel to the edge of the continent, with the magnetite series closer to the edge. Redox controls the ore minerals. As the slide explains it, magnetite-series magmas keep sulphur in the melt, so they can form tin ores rich in sulphide, with stannite. Ilmenite-series magmas lose sulphide early, so they form tin ores poor in sulphide and rich in cassiterite. Peraluminous means Al/(Na + K + 2Ca) > 1. The extra aluminium is in cordierite, garnet, muscovite, andalusite, sillimanite, topaz and biotite. S-type granites are usually peraluminous. slides 29 and 30

Concept 2 91 to 104 m

Volatiles and metal budgets

Volatiles are essential for these deposits. Water is the most common volatile. But a granite magma must be undersaturated in water (less than about 3 wt% H₂O) to rise high in the crust, because if it is saturated in water, it crystallises at depth. Chlorine carries Sn, W, Mo, Sb and other metals (Cu, Pb, Zn, Ag) as chloride complexes. Lithium, boron and fluorine lower the melting temperature and let fractionation continue to low temperatures. A normal granite might stop fractionating at about 500 °C, but a granite rich in these elements can continue below 300 °C. F and B also make H₂O and Cl more soluble in the melt, so volatiles separate at a lower pressure. This is why tourmaline, topaz and Li-micas are such good signs. slide 34 lecture recording, 26 Oct

The amount of metal needed is surprisingly normal. Crustal rocks contain 2 to 5 ppm Sn. Unaltered granites with tin mineralisation contain 10 to hundreds of ppm. So simple fractionation of a granite that is already rich in Sn is enough to make a deposit. Tungsten increases from 1 to 2 ppm in the crust to 10 to 15 ppm in fractionated granites. They are deposited in different ways. Tin is deposited by mixing with meteoric water, separation of vapour, reaction between fluid and wall rock, reaction with feldspars, and replacement of carbonate. So tin replacement deposits are common. Tungsten depends strongly on temperature: 99% of it is deposited between 350 and 300 °C. So tungsten replacement deposits are rare. slide 35 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Deposit scale 104 to 117 m

Greisens, veins and alteration

A is a granite altered by metasomatism. Hydrothermal fluid has replaced more than 90% of the feldspar with muscovite and quartz, often with topaz, tourmaline and cassiterite. Greisens range from veinlets millimetres wide to zones several metres across, and are usually medium to coarse grained. In New England, some become topazite, a rock made mostly of topaz. They usually form where a younger granite rich in fluid intrudes an older granite. The acidic fluids from the younger granite attack the feldspars of the older granite. So the ore is on the edges, in the greisen, not in the granite itself. slide 31 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Field task · a greisenslide 31

This rock was once granite. Which minerals are missing, and what replaced them?

Show what I see
  • A pale, granular rock, yellowish in some places, with no visible pink K-feldspar or white plagioclase.

The feldspars have gone, and quartz and muscovite have replaced them. The yellowish colour is the muscovite. This is a greisen, and tin and tungsten minerals are usually found in greisens. slide 31 lecture recording, 26 Oct

The mineralisation occurs as veins, sheeted veins (many parallel veins), lodes and breccia fill, and sometimes disseminated. Open cavities lined with euhedral crystals are common, with quartz crystals more than a metre long (as at Kingsgate in northern NSW). Veins extend from the granite into the hornfels around it, up to about a kilometre. So you may not see the granite at all. Much of the tin in Cornwall came from the hornfels. The veins have silicic, feldspathic and mica alteration zones around them. These extend vertically for hundreds of metres, and are zoned as temperature falls and distance from the vein increases. slides 32 and 33 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Case study 117 to 130 m

The Mole Granite

The Mole Granite in north-eastern NSW is a lens-shaped, sill-like body of about 650 km². It is Triassic (248 Ma) and part of the New England Batholith. It intruded Carboniferous and Permian sedimentary and volcanic rocks, and it contains more than 1200 small deposits. The tin is in the core, the tungsten is along the edge, and smaller amounts of Ag, Zn and Pb are in the country rocks. All of these are within the top 200 m of the exposed granite. Magmatic fluid from the granite mixed with meteoric water and deposited cassiterite, wolframite, tourmaline and other minerals, between 600 and 200 °C. This is the slider at the top of the page. slides 36 to 38 (Audétat et al., 1998, 2000)

The tin fields were once very busy. Tens of thousands of people worked the ground around Emmaville and Torrington, first for alluvial cassiterite and then for hard-rock ore. Torrington is now a ghost town. At Emmaville, miners found open cavities metres across, lined with possibly the best cassiterite crystals on quartz in the world. A student study of sulphur isotopes in the sulphides showed that the values became more magmatic closer to the granite. This is a way to tell how far you are from the source. slides 37 to 39 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Case study 130 to 144 m

Tallebung

Tallebung, in central NSW, is a Sn-W deposit that Sky Metals is exploring now. It was mined underground in the late 1800s and early 1900s for vein cassiterite and silver. Then, from about the 1930s to the 1980s, a private company washed the surface material away with water jets (sluicing). This removed the soil and left the veins very well exposed. The host rock is phyllite. The nearest granite is several kilometres away, but the deepest holes, at about 500 m, show the veins becoming more like granite. The ore is simple: cassiterite and ferberite in quartz with minor sulphides. They are coarse enough to separate by gravity after crushing. slides 40 to 46 lecture recording, 26 Oct

The black mineral is cassiterite (with ferberite). Both are coarse and very dense. So you crush the quartz and separate them by gravity, almost like panning. No complicated chemistry is needed. slide 42 lecture recording, 26 Oct

The old open cuts nearby were probably dug for silver. Goethite and hematite there replace sulphides and contain silver chlorides. This is supergene ore from the oxide zone (Lecture 2), which the early miners took before the tin. slides 43 and 44 lecture recording, 26 Oct

Memorise
  • Vein and greisen Sn-W-Mo from high-level (1.5 to 5 km), K-rich, highly fractionated granites; magnetite series (Mo-W) vs ilmenite series (Sn-W).
  • Volatiles: H₂O <3 wt% to rise; Cl transports metals; Li, B, F extend fractionation.
  • W precipitates at 350 to 300 °C; Sn by mixing, wall-rock and feldspar reaction, replacement.
  • Mole Granite: Sn core, W margin, Ag-Zn-Pb in country rock; 1200+ small deposits.
