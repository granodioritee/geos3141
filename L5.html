GEOS3141 Mineral and Energy Resources · Lecture 5 · T3 2026

Why broken rock often holds rich ore

Metals are found everywhere in the Earth's crust, but usually in amounts too small to mine. Geological structures, such as faults, shear zones and folds, can concentrate metals. They do this in three ways: they create open space, they guide fluids through the rock, and they trap those fluids. Later movement on a structure can also cut an orebody into pieces. In the 1800s, miners often followed a rich vein until it suddenly ended. They did not know where the rest of it had gone. In this lecture, you will learn how to find it.

What structures do

An ore deposit is an unusually high concentration of elements. To concentrate anything, you need a pathway and a trap. Structures provide both. They are also important for coal, oil and gas. Structures do four things (four jobs). Remember them, because every example in this lecture is one of these four. slide 2

Make spaceFaults, fractures and dilational jogs open gaps. Fluid can flow through these gaps, and minerals can grow in them.
Block flowSome structures act as seals. Examples are fault gouge (rock ground to clay), impermeable beds in fold hinges, and dykes. Fluid collects behind the seal and deposits its metals.
Make rock weakerDeformation and fluid together can change strong, competent rock into weak rock full of chlorite and talc. This is called rock softening.
Move oreA later fault can cut a deposit and move part of it metres to kilometres away. This is what happened in the game above.
Field note · dykes can act like damslecture recording, 22 Sep

In many epithermal and orogenic gold districts, there are mines with names like "Golden Dyke". Often, the dyke itself contains little gold. The dyke intruded before the gold event. Later, it acted as a barrier (an aquitard) to the fluid carrying gold. The fluid deposited its gold right against the edge of the dyke. So a structure does not have to be permeable to control ore. Sometimes its job is to stop the fluid.

Memorise
  • Structures make space, block flow, soften rock and move ore.
  • Concentrating anything needs a pathway and a trap; structures provide both.

Types of structures

Faults, shear zones, folds, bedding planes and contacts, foliations, veins, diatremes and breccias are all important for ore. One principle connects them: any surface that already exists is a plane of weakness. When stress is applied later, the rock moves again along this surface (reactivation), instead of breaking new rock. Even bedding counts. Beds next to each other have different composition, texture and grain size, so they break along the contact, and fluid can enter. This is also why geological contacts are hard to find exposed in the field: they weather first. slides 3 and 7

Field task · Rylstone Volcanics, NSWslide 8

These are ash beds with sandstone beds between them. Why are some layers more altered, and set back further into the cutting, than others?

Show the reasoning

The fine-grained ash beds are more altered, more weathered and more fractured. The coarser sandstone stands out. Fine ash reacts more easily, and the contacts between beds are planes of weakness. So fluid mainly flowed along certain layers and into them. Bedding was a structure that already existed, and it controlled where the alteration went.

Pre, syn and post: when did the structure form?

You can sort every structure by when it formed compared with the ore. This is the most useful idea in the lecture, because it tells you what the structure did to the deposit. slides 6 to 10

Timing animatorpre-mineralisation

Pre-mineralisation structures formed before the ore. They include bedding, older faults (such as growth faults around calderas), fractures, folds and earlier foliations. They move again (are reactivated) and become pathways for fluid. Syn-mineralisation structures form or move while the ore is being deposited. Veins are the most common, but faults in porphyry systems and soft-sediment slump folds in VMS and SEDEX systems can also be syn-mineralisation. Post-mineralisation structures formed after the ore. They cut and offset the ore and make the host rock weaker. When fluid flows through shear zones after the ore has formed, soft minerals like talc and chlorite grow. Miners call this . It is hard to support underground. Talc also floats together with the sulphides during flotation, which makes the concentrate less pure.

Memorise
  • Pre-mineralisation: bedding, older faults, folds, foliations, reactivated as plumbing.
  • Syn: form or move during ore deposition (veins, porphyry faults, slump folds in VMS and SEDEX).
  • Post: cut and offset ore; talc and chlorite shear zones make bad ground and spoil flotation.

Concept 3 38 to 54 m

From plates to the mine

Structures work at every scale: plate tectonic, regional, local or mine, hand specimen and microscope. Each scale answers a different exploration question.

Plate scale: which deposits are possible

Some deposit types only form in certain tectonic settings. VMS deposits need extension in an ocean basin, at a spreading ridge or a back-arc. Without extension there are no black smokers. Porphyry copper-gold-molybdenum deposits need a convergent margin where oceanic crust is subducted, to make their magmas. Some of the largest porphyries seem to form where seamounts were subducted. slides 11 and 12

Field task · the life of a basinslide 22

Match each phase to the deposits that form in it: rift, spreading, island arc, closing.

Show the answer
  • Rift: the rift itself forms little ore. But carbonatites from deep in the mantle can rise through continental rifts (rare earth elements).
  • Spreading: thin crust and melting caused by lower pressure (decompression melting): VMS and magmatic sulphide deposits.
  • Island arc: subduction magmas: porphyry and epithermal deposits.
  • Closing: collision and metamorphism: orogenic gold and many other deposits.

When the crust is pulled apart, it becomes thinner. Magma rises because of decompression, and fluids find easy pathways. This is why extension is important for so many deposit types.

Regional scale: the main pathways

The most important regional structures are tens of kilometres long. They are deep crustal faults (detachments) that reach the middle and lower crust and carry deep fluids upwards. They are essential for orogenic gold. But the key point is: the deposits are almost never on the large fault itself. They are on smaller second-order and third-order faults (splays) that branch off it. Other important regional structures are large folds, thrust faults, and regional unconformities, which are important for uranium. slide 13

Field task · faults of central NSWslide 14

Most of the major faults run roughly north-south. Why? How could you use this map to search for deposits?

Show the reasoning

Eastern Australia grew by accretion. New terranes were added from the east onto the craton in the west, so the main sutures and faults run roughly north-south. Deposits are often found where major faults cross smaller cross-faults. In the late 1970s and 1980s, Ed O'Driscoll, a geologist at Western Mining Corporation, chose exploration targets where lines (lineaments) crossed on satellite images. His ideas are seen as part of the path to the discovery of Olympic Dam. A large deposit needs a lot of fluid, and a lot of fluid needs a large structure.

Mine scale: the exact location

Structures smaller than about 1 km decide exactly where the ore is. These include third-order and fourth-order faults, favourable bed contacts (for example carbonate beds against siltstone or mudstone), fold hinges, and shear zones. They focus the fluid and stop it from escaping. Without this focusing, the metal spreads everywhere at low grade, and there is nothing to mine. slide 15

Concept 4 54 to 72 m

Three ways to deform rock

There are only three basic ways to deform a block of rock. You can pull it apart (extension, normal faults). You can push it together (compression, reverse and thrust faults, folds). Or you can slide it sideways (strike-slip). Each one makes different spaces and moves fluid in a different way, so each one makes different ore shapes. Try each one below.

Field task · same rocks, different stressslides 20 and 21

Compare the top diagram (extension) with the bottom diagram (compression). How does the shape of the ore body change, and why?

Show the reasoning

In extension, the fault opens a pathway. Meteoric water flows down through limestone and mixes with basin fluid, and one large lens of ore forms on the fault. In compression, the fold and the fault act as an aquitard (a barrier). Fluid is pushed up underneath it and replaces favourable beds, so a series of smaller lenses forms. The rocks are the same, but the stress is different, so the shape of the ore is completely different. Also, if there is compression in one part of an orogen, there is extension in another part.

These are computer models of an old fault under 0.66%, 3.96% and 10% extension (left) and shortening (right). What happens as the strain increases?

Show what I see

The strain (hot colours) collects around the fault that already existed, so the fault moves again (is reactivated). With more extension, second-order faults (F2) and then third-order faults (F3) form, and a larger graben develops. Compression produces an almost opposite pattern. More deformation means more fractures and more fluid flow. The famous Western Australian geologist David Groves said that the more complex the geology, the better the chance of a world-class deposit.

Field task · Carlin-type goldslide 23

Why does the gold spread sideways at certain levels, instead of staying on the fault?

Show the reasoning

The extensional fault is the pathway, and the silty limestone beds absorb the fluid like a sponge. Fluid rises up the fault and spreads sideways into favourable beds that react easily. It dissolves carbonate (decalcification) and replaces it with silica (jasperoid). The gold grains are micron-sized and invisible, so you explore for the alteration and the right host beds. Carlin deposits in Nevada have produced tens of millions of ounces of gold, and important deposits of this type are now known in southern China. Without extension, there would be no deposit.

Field task · strike-slipslide 27

Strike-slip movement forms stepped, S-shaped (sigmoidal) en echelon veins. In the goldfields of the Lachlan Fold Belt, are these the veins that contain gold?

Show the answer

Usually not. In the Lachlan Fold Belt, the sigmoidal en echelon veins are early D1 structures. They crystallised during movement (syn-deformational, rootless veins), and they usually contain no gold. The gold-bearing veins are much flatter and formed later. This is the timing idea again: know which generation of veins contains the gold.

Folds as traps: the same for oil and gold

A fold hinge is a zone of weakness. If an impermeable layer covers it, it is also a trap. This is true whether the fluid carries oil and gas or gold. Run both examples below.

Field task · the Zagros fold beltslide 26

The Arabian plate pushing into Iran formed a fold-thrust belt with many very large anticlines. Why does the oil not escape up the many faults?

Show the reasoning

There are two seals. First, shale layers in the fold hinges are impermeable, so oil that rises into the anticline is trapped under them. Second, fault movement grinds rock into clay gouge, and oil and gas cannot flow through clay. So many faults act as barriers, not as leaks. Structures control energy resources in the same way that they control metals.

Field task · orogenic gold in foldsslide 25

Find the three places where gold can be trapped in this compressional system.

Show the answer

Metamorphic fluids form at depth, where pyrrhotite changes to pyrite and releases arsenic, antimony and H2S. These fluids rise along faults and collect in three places. (1) Fold hinges, which are zones of weakness. (2) Saddle reefs, where a small amount of extension during folding opens gaps between beds at the hinge, as at Bendigo and Ballarat. (3) Replacement of reactive units, such as limestone or banded iron formation, at the top of the fold.

Concept 6 86 to 106 m

Yilgarn gold: the best place to learn about structural control

The Yilgarn Craton in Western Australia is probably the best example in the world of ore controlled by structures. It has Archaean greenstone belts (old basalt and sedimentary ocean-floor sequences). These are intruded by granites and cut by north-south shear zones, and they contain many gold deposits. The gold formed in a narrow range of conditions: about 300 to 500 °C and 2 to 4 kbar (upper greenschist to lower amphibolite facies). It formed late in the history of deformation and metamorphism, not at the peak of metamorphism. Most deposits formed at the brittle-ductile transition, roughly 5 to 15 km deep. slides 29 to 36 and lecture recording, 22 Sep

The pit is long and narrow, several kilometres long. What controls this shape? What does B tell you about why visible gold is rare here?

Show the reasoning

The pit follows a structural corridor: a group of lodes hosted in shear zones. So the pit is long in the direction of strike. It has been mined since the 1890s, and as the pit grew, it removed the old underground tunnels. B shows a quartz vein with gold. But on the Golden Mile, most gold is fine-grained and invisible, so a sample like B is unusual. Kalgoorlie is one of the richest gold fields in the world. lecture recording

The key rule for exploration in the Yilgarn: gold is found on second-order and third-order structures, which are splays and cross-faults that branch off the large shear zones. It is also found on the edges of granites. It is not found on the first-order shear zone itself. The large structure is the main pathway. The small structures are the places where fluid slowed down, mixed or met reactive rock. The larger first-order faults are usually in compression, so fluid cannot move through them easily. The smaller splays are where extension opened space. Test this idea below.

The whole deposit plunges steeply to a great depth, but the individual quartz veins and lenses in C are almost horizontal. Steeply plunging ore shoots made of flat veins are very typical of orogenic gold. These systems go very deep. With today's high gold prices, it is now profitable to mine deep levels that were not profitable a few years ago, even though deep mining is expensive (for example, the air must be cooled).

Field task · Granny Smith, Yilgarnslides 38 and 39

The same granite and the same tectonic setting occur in other parts of the belt, with no deposit. Here, all the mineralisation is on one side of the granite margin. Why only one side?

Show the reasoning

The granite acted as a rheological barrier. It is a strong, rigid body that fluid could not pass through. So fluid collected and deposited gold along the structures against one side of the granite. Local structure decides whether a deposit forms, not the regional setting. This is why two areas that look the same at regional scale can be very different. Porphyry dykes and banded iron formation also help to concentrate ore here.

Field task · the crustal continuumslide 33

One shear system, many depths. What is the main idea?

Show the reasoning

This is the crustal continuum model. The same type of fluid system can deposit gold at many depths along one connected set of pathways, from deep, hot granulite facies up to shallow sub-greenschist facies. The style of the deposit changes with depth: ductile shear-hosted ore at depth, and brittle veins and breccias at shallow levels. But it is one system. The largest Yilgarn deposits, including Kalgoorlie, are in the upper greenschist part of the profile.

Field task · where did the gold come from?slide 41

A specimen like this makes you ask: where did all this gold come from?

Show the answer

There is no single answer. There were several gold events of different ages, and probably several sources. (1) Granites: gold is concentrated in the last melts and fluids of the granite. (2) Basalts: fluid collects small amounts of gold from very large volumes of basalt and carries it to shear zones. (3) An original contribution from the mantle. Here is a good example of timing: at some nickel mines, gold is found in the same structures as the nickel. But the gold came in during a later orogenic event, long after the nickel ore formed with its host rocks.

Concept 7 106 to 120 m

Pilbara: domes rise, greenstones sink

The Pilbara Craton has a different structural history. It does not have long regional shear zones with splays. Instead, very large granite domes rose upwards, like blobs in a lava lamp. These are called . The dense greenstones between the domes sank. This sinking stretched the rock vertically and formed . The gold is in the shear zones on the sides of the domes, between the granite and the sinking greenstone. So in the Pilbara, you look between the granite domes, and some gold is even hosted in the granite. slides 44 to 49
