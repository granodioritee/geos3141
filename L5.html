GEOS3141 Mineral and Energy Resources · Lecture 5 · T3 2026

Why broken rock makes rich rock

Metals are everywhere in the crust, spread too thinly to mine. Structures are one of nature's best tools for gathering them: faults, shears and folds open space, channel fluid and trap it, and later they can slice an orebody in half. Old miners in the 1800s would follow a rich vein until it simply stopped, with no idea where it had gone. You're about to do better than them.

What structures actually do

An ore deposit is an anomalous concentration of elements, and concentrating anything needs a pathway and a trap. Structures provide both. They also matter for coal, oil and gas. Hold onto four jobs, because every example in this lecture is one of them. slide 2

Make spaceFaults, fractures and dilational jogs open gaps for fluid to flow through and minerals to grow in.
Block flowSome structures seal: fault gouge ground to clay, impermeable beds in fold hinges, dykes. Fluid ponds behind them and drops its load.
Soften rockDeformation plus fluid turns strong, competent rock into weak rock full of chlorite and talc. That's rock softening.
Move oreA later fault can slice a deposit and shift part of it metres to kilometres away, which is exactly what happened in the game above.
Field note · dykes as damslecture recording, 22 Sep

In many epithermal and orogenic gold districts you'll find mines called things like "Golden Dyke". Often the dyke itself carries little gold. It intruded before the gold event and then acted as a barrier (an aquitard) to the gold-bearing fluid, which dumped its gold right against the dyke contact. A structure doesn't have to be permeable to control ore; sometimes its job is to stop the fluid.

Memorise
  • Structures make space, block flow, soften rock and move ore.
  • Concentrating anything needs a pathway and a trap; structures provide both.

The cast of structures

Faults, shears, folds, bedding planes and contacts, foliations, veins, diatremes and breccias all matter for ore. One principle ties them together: any existing surface is a plane of weakness, and later stress will reactivate it rather than break fresh rock. Even bedding counts, because neighbouring beds differ in composition, texture and grain size, so they fail along the contact and let fluid in. That's also why geological contacts are so hard to find exposed in the field: they weather out first. slides 3 and 7

Field task · Rylstone Volcanics, NSWslide 8

These are interbedded ash beds and sandstone. Why are some layers more altered and recessed than others?

Show the reasoning

The fine-grained ash beds are more altered, weathered back and fractured, while the coarser sandstone stands out. Fine ash is more reactive and the bed contacts are planes of weakness, so fluid preferentially went along and into particular layers. Bedding was a pre-existing structure that controlled where alteration went.

Concept 2 24 to 38 m

Pre, syn and post: when did the structure form?

Every structure can be sorted by its timing relative to the ore. This is the most useful framework in the lecture, because it tells you what a structure did to the deposit. slides 6 to 10

Timing animatorpre-mineralisation

Pre-mineralisation structures include bedding, older faults (such as growth faults around calderas), fractures, folds and earlier foliations. They get reactivated and become the plumbing. Syn-mineralisation structures form or move while ore is being deposited. Veins are the commonest, but faults in porphyry systems and sedimentary slump folds in VMS and SEDEX systems can be syn too. Post-mineralisation structures cut and offset the ore and weaken the host. Shear zones that carry fluid after the ore grow soft phyllosilicates like talc and chlorite, which miners call : hard to support underground, and talc also floats with the sulphides during flotation, contaminating the concentrate.

Memorise
  • Pre-mineralisation: bedding, older faults, folds, foliations, reactivated as plumbing.
  • Syn: form or move during ore deposition (veins, porphyry faults, slump folds in VMS and SEDEX).
  • Post: cut and offset ore; talc and chlorite shear zones make bad ground and spoil flotation.

Concept 3 38 to 54 m

From plates to the mine

Structures work at every scale: plate tectonic, regional, local or mine, hand specimen, microscope. Each scale answers a different exploration question.

Plate scale decides what's possible

Some deposit types only exist in particular tectonic settings. VMS deposits need extension in an ocean basin, at a spreading ridge or a back-arc, because without it there are no black smokers. Porphyry copper-gold-molybdenum deposits need a convergent margin with subducting oceanic crust to make their magmas, and some of the very largest porphyries seem to sit where seamounts were subducted. slides 11 and 12

Field task · a basin's life storyslide 22

Match each phase to the deposits it favours: rift, spreading, island arc, closing.

Show the answer
  • Rift: little ore from the rift itself, but deep mantle-derived carbonatites can come up through continental rifts (rare earths).
  • Spreading: thinned crust and decompression melting: VMS and magmatic sulphide deposits.
  • Island arc: subduction magmas: porphyry and epithermal.
  • Closing: collision and metamorphism: orogenic gold and many others.

Pull the crust apart and you thin it, so magma rises by decompression and fluids find easy paths. That's why extension features in so many deposit types.

Regional scale: the highways

The most important regional structures are tens of kilometres long: deep crustal faults (detachments) that reach mid- to lower-crustal levels and carry deep fluids up. They're essential for orogenic gold. But here's the key point: the deposits almost never sit on the big fault itself. They sit on smaller second- and third-order faults (splays) branching off it. Other regional structures that matter: large folds, thrust faults, and regional unconformities, which are big for uranium. slide 13

Field task · faults of central NSWslide 14

Most of the major faults run roughly north-south. Why, and how could you use this map to hunt for deposits?

Show the reasoning

Eastern Australia grew by accretion: terranes were added from the east onto the craton in the west, so the main sutures and faults are oriented roughly north-south. Deposits cluster where major faults intersect minor cross-faults. In the late 1970s and 1980s one Western Mining Corporation geologist, Ed O'Driscoll, built his targeting on intersecting lineaments in satellite images, and that thinking is credited as part of the path to Olympic Dam. A big deposit needs a lot of fluid, and a lot of fluid needs a big structure.

Mine scale: where exactly

Structures under about 1 km decide exactly where the ore sits: third- and fourth-order faults, favourable bed contacts (carbonate beds against siltstone or mudstone), fold hinges and noses, and shear zones. They focus and cap the fluid. Without that focusing the metal just disseminates everywhere at low grade and you have nothing mineable. slide 15

Concept 4 54 to 72 m

Three ways to deform rock

You can only do three basic things to a block of rock: pull it apart (extension, normal faults), push it together (compression, reverse and thrust faults, folds), or slide it sideways (strike-slip). Each makes different space and moves fluid differently, so each makes different ore geometries. Scrub through each one.

Deformation labextension

Field task · same rocks, different stressslides 20 and 21

Compare the top (extension) and bottom (compression) diagrams. How does the ore body change shape, and why?

Show the reasoning

In extension, the fault opens a pathway, meteoric water flows down through limestone and mixes with basinal fluid, and you get one large lens on the fault. In compression the fold and fault act as an aquitard: fluid is squeezed up beneath it and replaces favourable beds, giving a series of smaller lenses. Same rocks, different stress regime, completely different ore geometry. And if there's compression somewhere in an orogen, there's extension somewhere else.

These are numerical models of an old fault under 0.66%, 3.96% and 10% extension (left) and shortening (right). What happens as strain increases?

Show what I see

Strain (hot colours) bunches around the pre-existing fault, so it's reactivated. With more extension, second-order (F2) and then third-order (F3) faults appear and a bigger graben develops; compression produces an almost opposite geometry. More deformation means more fractures and more fluid flow. As the famous Western Australian geologist David Groves put it, the more complex the geology, the better the chance of a world-class deposit.

Field task · Carlin-type goldslide 23

Why does the gold spread sideways at certain levels instead of staying on the fault?

Show the reasoning

The extensional fault is the pipe; the silty limestone beds are the sponge. Fluid rises up the fault and reacts laterally with favourable, reactive beds, dissolving carbonate (decalcification) and replacing it with silica (jasperoid). The gold is micron-sized and invisible, so you explore for the alteration and the right host beds. Carlin deposits in Nevada have produced tens of millions of ounces, and important ones are now known in southern China. Without extension, there'd be no deposit.

Field task · strike-slipslide 27

Strike-slip movement produces stepped, sigmoidal en echelon veins. In the Lachlan Fold Belt goldfields, are those the gold-bearing veins?

Show the answer

Usually not. There, the sigmoidal en echelon veins are early D1 structures, crystallised during movement (syn-deformational, rootless veins), and they're generally barren. The gold-bearing veins are much flatter and formed later. That's the timing framework again: know which vein generation carries the gold.

Concept 5 72 to 86 m

Folds as traps: oil and gold play the same game

A fold hinge is a zone of weakness and, if it's capped by an impermeable layer, a trap. That's true whether the fluid carries hydrocarbons or gold. Run both.

Hinge trapZagros oil

Field task · the Zagros fold beltslide 26

Arabia pushing into Iran built a fold-thrust belt full of huge anticlines. Why don't the oil fields leak out up the many faults?

Show the reasoning

Two seals. Shale horizons in the fold hinges are impermeable, so oil rising into the anticline is trapped beneath them. And fault movement grinds rock to clay gouge, which neither oil nor gas can flow through, so many faults act as barriers rather than leaks. Structures control energy resources exactly as they control metals.

Field task · orogenic gold in foldsslide 25

Find the three places gold can be trapped in this compressional system.

Show the answer

Metamorphic fluids generated at depth (where pyrrhotite converts to pyrite, releasing arsenic, antimony and H2S) rise along faults and collect in: fold hinges, the zones of weakness; saddle reefs, where slight extension during folding opens gaps between beds at the hinge, as at Bendigo and Ballarat; and replacement of reactive units such as limestone or banded iron formation at the top of the fold.

Concept 6 86 to 106 m

Yilgarn gold: the world's best structural classroom

Western Australia's Yilgarn Craton is arguably the best example on Earth of structurally controlled ore. Archaean greenstone belts (old basalt and sedimentary ocean-floor sequences), intruded by granites, sliced by north-south shear zones, carry gold deposit after deposit. The gold formed in a narrow window: about 300 to 500 °C and 2 to 4 kbar, upper greenschist to lower amphibolite facies, late in the deformation and metamorphic history rather than at peak metamorphism. Most deposits formed at the brittle-ductile transition, very roughly 5 to 15 km down. slides 29 to 36 and lecture recording, 22 Sep

Field task · the Kalgoorlie Superpitslide 28
A · the Superpit, Golden Mile
B · the target

The pit is long and narrow, kilometres in length. What controls that shape, and what does B tell you about why visible gold is rare here?

Show the reasoning

The pit follows a structural corridor: a cluster of shear-zone-hosted lodes, so the excavation is elongate along strike. It has been mined since the 1890s, and old underground workings have been swallowed as the pit grew. B is a quartz vein with gold, but on the Golden Mile most gold is fine-grained and invisible, so a sample like B is the exception rather than the rule. Kalgoorlie is one of the richest gold fields on Earth. lecture recording

The rule that makes Yilgarn exploration work: gold sits on second- and third-order structures, splays and cross-faults off the big shear zones, and on the margins of granites, not on the first-order shear zone itself. The big structure is the highway; the small ones are the off-ramps where fluid slowed down, mixed or met reactive rock. Larger first-order faults tend to be in compression, so fluid can't move through them easily; the smaller splays are where extension opened space. Test that.

Field task · Granny Smith, Yilgarnslides 38 and 39

The same granite and the same tectonic setting occur elsewhere in the belt with no deposit. Here, the mineralisation is all on one side of the granite margin. Why one side only?

Show the reasoning

The granite behaved as a rheological barrier: a strong, rigid body that fluid couldn't pass through, so fluid ponded and deposited gold along the structures against its margin on one side. Local structure, not the regional setting, decides whether you get a deposit, which is why two areas that look identical at regional scale can be so different. Porphyry dykes and banded iron formation also localise ore here.

Field task · the crustal continuumslide 33

One shear system, many depths. What's the idea here?

Show the reasoning

The crustal continuum model: the same kind of fluid system can deposit gold anywhere from deep, hot granulite facies up to shallow sub-greenschist facies along one connected plumbing system. The style of the deposit changes with depth (ductile shear-hosted deep, brittle veins and breccias shallow), but it's one system. The biggest Yilgarn deposits, Kalgoorlie included, sit in the upper greenschist part of the profile.

Field task · where did the gold come from?slide 41

A spectacular specimen like this begs the question: where did all this gold come from?

Show the answer

There's no single answer. There were multiple gold events of different ages and probably multiple sources: granites, where gold concentrates in late fractionated melts and fluids; basalts, from which fluid scavenges small amounts over huge volumes and delivers it to shear zones; and an original mantle contribution. One nice proof of timing: at some nickel mines, gold turns up in the same structures but came in during a later orogenic event, long after the nickel ore formed with its host rocks.

Concept 7 106 to 120 m

Pilbara: domes rise, greenstones sink

The Pilbara Craton tells a different structural story. Instead of long regional shear zones with splays, huge granite domes rose up like blobs in a lava lamp (), and the dense greenstones between them sank. That sinking stretched the rock vertically, producing , and the shear zones on the flanks of the domes, between the granite and the sinking greenstone, are where the gold is. So in the Pilbara you look between the granite domes, and some gold is even granite-hosted. slides 44 to 49
