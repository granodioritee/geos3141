The big picture

This lecture uses many new words very quickly: syngenetic, epigenetic, hypogene, supergene, metallotect, mesothermal. Do not try to memorise them as a list. Remember one sentence instead: an ore deposit is an unusually high concentration of elements that are normally spread thinly through the crust. This is also why deposits are hard to find. Every other idea in Lecture 1 answers one of three questions.

Question 1: when does a concentration count as ore? This is an economic question. To answer it you need grade, tonnage, resources, reserves and even politics. Question 2: how much must nature concentrate a metal? The answer is the concentration factor. It explains why some deposits are common and others are rare. Question 3: which processes can concentrate a metal? The main ones are tectonic setting, magma type and hydrothermal fluids. Every deposit lecture later in the course uses these processes, so it is worth learning them well now.

Concept 1 12 to 24 m

Ore is an economic word, not a rock type

The formal definition is: ore is any naturally occurring material from which a mineral or aggregate of value can be extracted at a profit. The key words are "at a profit". Imagine a rock with 0.3 per cent copper. At a high copper price it is ore. At a low copper price it is waste. The rock itself does not change at all. So a rock is never simply "ore". It is ore only for a certain price, cost, technology and set of laws. Try it with the sliders below.

The rocks did not change, but the amount of ore did. The same idea decides which minerals are and which are . An ore mineral is the mineral you sell. Gangue is the worthless mineral that comes out of the ground with it. You must pay to crush it, process it and throw it away. Common gangue minerals are quartz, carbonates, chlorite, micas and pyrite. But a mineral that is usually gangue can sometimes be an ore mineral.

Real deposit · Mount Isa pyriteslide 15

In most sulphide deposits pyrite is a problem. It has no value, it makes acid, and it lowers the grade of the concentrate. At Mount Isa, however, pyrite has been treated as an ore mineral. The reason is Phosphate Hill, about 150 km to the south. Phosphate Hill needs sulphuric acid to turn phosphate rock into fertiliser. When you heat (roast) pyrite, it gives off sulphur dioxide, and sulphur dioxide can be made into sulphuric acid. The mineral and the geology are the same. The only difference is that a nearby buyer will pay for it.

Ore can also be divided into three kinds, based on whether you can see the valuable mineral. Bulk commodities, such as iron ore and manganese ore, are almost pure mineral. They are mined in hundreds of millions of tonnes. Sulphide ores are rich in metal and you can see the ore minerals. For example, a galena-rich specimen may contain about 10 per cent lead. Invisible ores include most gold deposits. Their grade is only a few grams per tonne, so you almost never see the gold. The Witwatersrand conglomerates are the best example: they hold more gold than any other deposit, but the gold is invisible. For this reason, mines have their own laboratories for . After every blast, the broken rock is sampled and assayed. You cannot tell ore from waste by eye, and processing waste by mistake is expensive.

Field note · "economic" now includes politicslecture recording, 14 Sep

In the last three to five years the industry has changed. Before, price was the only thing that mattered. Now governments also pay for deposits that would not be profitable on price alone, because they want a secure supply. Tungsten is a good example. Tungsten is needed for hardened military projectiles. China produces most of the world's tungsten and has limited its exports. As a result, the US military is now investing in Australian tungsten. The opposite has happened to Australian nickel. Indonesia now produces large amounts of cheap nickel from laterite deposits and processes it at the mine. This has made it hard for Australian nickel mines to compete.

For the same reason, the term can be misleading. No mineral is critical by itself. A commodity is critical for a country when that country needs it but does not control the supply. This is why each country has a different list.

Concept 2 24 to 36 m

Concentration factors: how much nature must concentrate a metal

Every element has an average abundance in the continental crust. To make an ore deposit, a process must collect the element from a very large volume of normal rock. It must then put it into a small volume, at a grade high enough to mine. The is the minimum economic grade divided by the crustal average. A high factor means a deposit is less likely to form.

Aluminium already makes up 8.2 per cent of the crust. You only need to concentrate it about 4 times to make bauxite. Strong tropical weathering does this by removing the other elements. Tin is only about 2 parts per million in the crust, but you need about 0.5 per cent to mine it. So tin must be concentrated about 2500 times. Only a few processes can do this, mainly highly evolved, reduced granites and their fluids. This is why tin deposits are rare, while iron and aluminium deposits are quite easy to find.

The average gold content of the crust is 4 ppb. A typical mining grade is 5 g/t.

5 g/t = 5 g per 1 000 000 g = 5 ppm = 5000 ppb 5000 ppb ÷ 4 ppb = 1250×

The key fact is: 1 g/t is exactly 1 ppm, because one tonne is one million grams. When you know this, each row in the table is a simple division.

Here is a question to think about. Zinc needs a bigger concentration factor than nickel, but zinc deposits are much more common. Why? The factor only tells you how much concentration is needed. It does not tell you how often nature does it. Zinc is easily carried by warm, salty fluids, and these fluids flow through sedimentary basins all the time. Most nickel sulphide ore forms only when a sulphide liquid separates from a very hot ultramafic magma. This happens much less often. So use the table to see how much concentration is needed. Then ask which processes can do it, and how often they happen.

Memorise

  • Copper: 1% is 10 000 ppm; crust about 55 ppm; so about 180 times.

Concept 3 36 to 48 m

Grade, tonnage, and what an assay is

Every deposit is described by two numbers. is the amount of ore. is the amount of metal in each tonne. Tonnage × grade gives the contained metal, and this is the number the market cares about. The units depend on the commodity. Precious metals (gold, silver, the platinum group elements) use grams per tonne. Base metals (copper, lead, zinc, tungsten) use weight per cent. Oxide ores, such as iron, manganese, uranium and rare earths, use weight per cent of the oxide. Diamonds use carats per cubic metre, because a rock contains very little diamond (1 carat is 0.2 g). Grade is an average of one-metre samples

A reported grade is the average of many drill assays. In Australia, the standard method is to cut the drill core in half. One half is crushed and sent for , in one-metre intervals. You must sample every metre in the same way, even if you cannot see any mineralisation. If you only sampled the parts that look rich, the average grade would be too high and the deposit would look bigger than it is. Also, grade is an economic term, so waste rock does not have a grade.

Within a deposit, people talk about low, moderate, high and very high grade zones. In gold deposits the highest grades are called grades. These are usually over 100 g/t. An epithermal or orogenic gold system with an average of 2 g/t can have small patches over 1000 g/t.

Field note · do not rely on bonanza gradeslecture recording, 14 Sep

When you model a deposit, leave out the bonanza intercepts or reduce them to a maximum value. They are real, but you cannot predict where they are. A mine plan based on them will promise more metal than you will really recover. Reducing very high values to a maximum is called a . A quick question: is 2.5 g/t gold a high or a low grade? Today it is quite good. Some mines work at 0.5 g/t or less.

Concept 4 48 to 60 m

From drill hole to reserve: steps of confidence

One drill hole with mineralisation does not make a mine. A deposit must go up a series of steps, and each step means more knowledge and more confidence. In Australia, the name of each step has a legal meaning under the . The lowest step is exploration results, which are the assays from drill holes. When you have enough holes to estimate tonnage and grade, you have a . A resource has three levels. An inferred resource is based on holes that are far apart, so you are assuming the mineralisation continues between them. Be careful when a company reports an inferred resource, because it may be too optimistic. An indicated resource has holes close enough together that it is reasonable to assume continuity. A measured resource has enough data to know the tonnage and grade with high confidence.

Going from a resource to an is a different kind of step. A reserve is the part of a resource that you have shown can be mined at a profit. To show this, you apply the : mining method, metallurgy, economics, marketing, legal, environmental, social and government factors. An indicated resource can become a probable reserve. A measured resource can become a proved reserve. It becomes only probable if one of the factors is still uncertain. An inferred resource can never become a reserve directly. The best way to understand this is to run a drilling program yourself.

Remember two practical points. First, it usually takes 10 to 15 years to go from discovery to mining, and the cost is very high. For example, a porphyry project on the border of Argentina and Chile is spending billions of dollars on a railway, power and housing before mining any ore. Second, most discoveries today are made by small and medium-sized companies. They cannot pay to build a mine, so they sell the deposit to a large company or form a with one. But once a mine is running, the profit can be very large. For example, if iron ore sells for about US$100/t and costs about US$30/t to produce, the margin is about 70 per cent.

Concept 5 60 to 72 m

Timing, direction and depth: three questions to ask about any ore

Most definitions in this lecture are really answers to three questions. Ask these questions every time you look at a piece of ore.

When did it form? Syngenetic or epigenetic

ore formed at the same time as the rock around it. Examples are a placer gravel, or a massive sulphide layer that was deposited on the seafloor together with the sediment. ore formed later than the host rock. Every vein is epigenetic, because the rock must already be solid before it can crack and the crack can fill. This matters for exploration. Syngenetic ore follows the stratigraphy, so you follow a favourable layer. Epigenetic ore follows the pathways that fluids used, so you follow structures. There are also three words for the shape of ore. Stratiform ore is a layer within the sediment. Stratabound ore stays within one rock unit, but it may have replaced that unit later. Discordant ore cuts across the layering.

Which way was the water moving? Hypogene or supergene

ore was deposited by fluids rising from depth. ore was formed by water moving down during weathering. This water dissolves metals near the surface and deposits them again lower down. Supergene enrichment can make a deposit much more valuable. The Endeavor mine at Cobar is a good example: supergene enrichment made zones of native silver, rich enough to change the economics of the mine. Lecture 2 covers this in detail.

How deep and how hot? Epithermal, mesothermal, hypothermal

These three words describe hydrothermal deposits by the depth and temperature where they formed. They come from Lindgren's classification, which is about 100 years old. The word "epithermal" is still used every day. Drag the probe down through the crust and see what changes.

Minerals as thermometers · which zone?

A useful habit is to use minerals as thermometers. Chalcopyrite usually needs more than about 200 °C. So if a sample has a lot of chalcopyrite, it is probably not epithermal. If a vein has only galena, sphalerite and fine banded quartz, it probably formed in the shallow, cooler zone. The 200 °C line is a general guide, not a strict rule. Tectonics comes first

Before you look at one particular hill, first check that you are in the right kind of crust. Each deposit type needs a certain combination of heat, fluid, magma and stress. Plate tectonics controls this combination. So the first step of exploration is to ask two questions. Which tectonic setting does this deposit type need? Where is that setting preserved today? You will often hear two words: and . Brownfield exploration is near existing or old mines, such as Cobar. Greenfield exploration is in areas where nothing has been found before. Olympic Dam is the most famous greenfield discovery. The exploration team was testing an idea about sediment-hosted copper. Instead, they found a completely new type of deposit, iron oxide copper-gold (IOCG), and it is one of the largest deposits in the world. The lesson: a good geological idea, tested carefully, can find deposits that a standard model would miss.

Australia's special problem: cover

Australia has not had glaciers recently. Glaciers scrape the land and expose fresh bedrock. Instead, most of Australia is covered by and transported sediment, in some places more than 100 metres thick. One industry estimate says that about 99 per cent of Australian exploration holes are less than 100 metres deep. Together, these two facts mean that much of Australia has not been properly explored. Even around Orange, an area that has been farmed for a long time, new porphyry deposits are still being found when people explore in new ways.

Concept 7 84 to 96 m

Magma type controls the metals

In second year you learned about tholeiitic, calc-alkaline and alkaline magma series in petrology. They are important here because each series carries a different group of metals. If you know the magma type, you know which deposits are possible.

The metals depend on where each magma comes from. Tholeiitic magmas are the closest to mantle composition. They include basalts from mid-ocean ridges and young arcs, and large mafic intrusions. They are low in potassium and have about 53 per cent silica. Mantle melts carry chromium, nickel, platinum group elements, titanium and vanadium. The main way these metals are concentrated is by crystals or sulphide droplets separating from the melt. Examples are the Bushveld chromitites and the Sudbury nickel-copper sulphides. In these deposits, later hydrothermal fluids are much less important.

Calc-alkaline magmas form above subduction zones. Water from the subducting slab rises into the mantle wedge and causes it to melt, and some crust is often mixed in. These magmas are wet and oxidised. When they crystallise in the upper crust, they release large volumes of fluid that carries metals. This is how porphyry copper-gold-molybdenum deposits, skarns and many epithermal deposits form. In these deposits, the hydrothermal stage is the most important.

Alkaline magmas include shoshonites, syenites, carbonatites, kimberlites and lamproites. They form by a small amount of melting deep in the mantle, in rifts, cratons and some arcs. A small amount of melt collects the incompatible elements. So these magmas give rare earth elements, phosphate, some iron oxide ores and, from kimberlites and lamproites, diamonds. If you are looking for primary rare earth deposits, do not look in tholeiitic or calc-alkaline rocks. Look for alkaline rocks. Now sort the deposits yourself.

          The hydrothermal chain: if one link fails, there is no deposit

          Most deposits in this course are hydrothermal, at least partly. So you need a clear picture of what is needed to form one. Think of it as a chain with six links. You need a source of metal, a fluid that can carry the metal, a pathway that focuses the fluid, a trap where the metal is deposited, a seal that keeps the fluid there long enough, and preservation of the deposit afterwards. If any link fails, there is no deposit. Also remember this rule: every deposit is structurally controlled. Run the simulation below. Then turn off one link at a time and see what goes wrong.

          Most of the chemistry happens in the trap, and this idea will come back many times. Hydrothermal fluids are usually undersaturated in metals. This means they carry less metal than they could. They only deposit metal when conditions change quickly. For example, the fluid cools, the pressure drops and the fluid boils, it mixes with cooler groundwater, or it reacts with a rock that neutralises its acid (limestone is the best example). The fluid leaves only a little metal each time it passes. So a very large volume of fluid must flow through the same small volume of rock for a long time. This is why focused structures and seals are so important.

          Worked example · can a magma really supply that much water?slide 44

          One cubic kilometre of quartz monzonite magma with 3 wt% water is said to release about 100 Mt of water. This happens when the magma rises to about 3.5 km depth, where water starts to separate (exsolve) from the melt. Check this number with second-year values:

          Mass ≈ 1 × 10⁹ m³ × 2600 kg/m³ ≈ 2.6 × 10¹² kg ≈ 2600 Mt Water ≈ 3% of 2600 Mt ≈ 78 Mt

          The answer is about 80 Mt, which is close to 100 Mt. Porphyry intrusions are often tens of cubic kilometres in size. So the magma alone can supply billions of tonnes of hot fluid that carries metal.

          Alteration: a bigger target than the ore

          When these fluids move through rock, they react with it. They change its minerals, texture and chemistry. This is called , which is a type of . Alteration is important for exploration because the alteration zone (halo) is much larger than the ore, often 10 to 100 times larger or more. Geophysical and geochemical surveys usually detect the alteration first. So the method is: find the alteration, then follow it inwards to the ore.

          Where the water comes from

          Water from magma is only one source. Fluids can also be water, seawater, water or metamorphic water. Real systems often mix several sources. You can think of it as a range. At the hot end, metals separate directly from magma (chromite, Ni-Cu sulphides). In the middle, fluids separate from a cooling magma (pegmatites, porphyries). At the cool end, these fluids mix with meteoric water or seawater (epithermal, SEDEX and exhalative deposits). A single system can move through the whole range as it cools.

          Why deposits are hard to classify, and why scale matters

          Early classifications, such as Lindgren's, grouped deposits by what you could see. Later classifications (Meyer, Guilbert and Park, Misra, Robb) became genetic. This means they group deposits by the process that formed them. Some deposit names are still not genetic. Carlin, Mississippi Valley type and Witwatersrand are still named after places, mainly because geologists still disagree about how they formed.

          Classification is difficult because nature does not follow our categories. A magmatic system becomes hydrothermal as it cools. Buried sediments release fluids, and these fluids alter and mineralise the sediments. Deformation creates new pathways, and fluids use old weak zones again, so one area can be mineralised several times by unrelated events. So do not depend too much on classification. "Porphyry" is a useful short name, but every real deposit is different, and you should not force it to match the textbook model.

          Real deposit · Argyle, and the danger of one modellecture recording, 14 Sep

          The model used at De Beers said that economic diamonds only occur in kimberlite. Because of this, the Argyle area was not properly followed up. Argyle turned out to be hosted by . It became the world's largest diamond producer by volume, and the main source of pink diamonds until it closed in 2020. A model is a tool that helps you find the next deposit. If you treat a model as a strict definition, you will miss the deposits that do not fit it.

          Look at every scale

          Study a deposit at every scale you can: metallogenic province, district, mine, outcrop and microscope. Each scale answers a different question. Is the tectonic setting right? Where were the fluid pathways? What was the order of events? Can the ore minerals be processed? Kelian in Kalimantan shows why this matters. It is an epithermal gold deposit hosted by a , but geologists did not fully understand this until the deposit had been mined out.

          Three more terms belong here. A is any feature that helped to concentrate metals, such as a fault, a reactive rock unit or a certain intrusion. A metallogenic province is a region with a typical group of deposits. A metallogenic epoch is a period of time that was favourable for forming deposits. For example, the Ordovician Macquarie Arc in central NSW is both the province and the epoch for porphyry copper-gold deposits in the state.
