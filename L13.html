GEOS3141 Mineral and Energy Resources · Lecture 13 · T3 2026

Gold from deep in mountain belts

Orogenic gold made Kalgoorlie, Bendigo, Ballarat and Hill End, and it has supplied most of the gold that humans have ever mined. It forms in quartz veins along faults in deformed, metamorphosed rock, late in mountain building. It can form at almost any depth from 2 to 20 km. This last point, the crustal continuum, is the key to this type of deposit. Move down through the crust and see how one fluid makes different deposits.

What orogenic gold is

deposits are gold deposits controlled by structures. They form during regional metamorphism and the deformation that comes with it, during orogenesis. Orogenesis is mountain building, under compression or transpression (compression with sideways movement). Böhlke first used the term in a 1982 USGS report about gold-quartz veins in metamorphic rocks. It became popular when David Groves at UWA used it from 1993, in his "crustal continuum" model for the lode gold deposits of the Yilgarn. slides 3 and 4 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Notice the older names in this history: metamorphic-hosted (the host rocks are metamorphosed), crustal continuum (they form at every level of the crust) and lode gold (the gold is in veins, also called lodes). In the past, about 75% of all gold ever mined came from orogenic deposits, if you include the Witwatersrand with them, as some people do. Today they supply about a third of world production. slides 4 and 5 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Concept 1 13 to 26 m

The recipe

Orogenic gold is defined as "structurally controlled vein systems or lodes" (Groves et al., 1998). The definition now also includes the disseminated gold in the alteration zone around the veins, which old prospectors ignored. It forms at continental margins under compression to transpression. It is mostly in metamorphic rocks (greenstones, granites, metasediments) on the ocean side of the arc, at 200 to 700 °C and 2 to 20 km. It forms from dilute fluids rich in CO₂ ± CH₄ and N₂. Quartz veins occur with carbonate, sericite and reduced minerals such as arsenopyrite. Ages range from Archean through Proterozoic and Palaeozoic to Mesozoic. slide 11

Remember four points. (1) Most gold comes during the retrograde change from amphibolite to greenschist facies, as the rocks cool. (2) The type of fault does not matter, as long as a structure was active when the fluid came through. (3) Despite the name, most mineralisation comes up to 100 million years after the main mountain building, late in the orogenic cycle. (4) High fluid pressure is essential. It drives fluid up through the crust and causes the earthquakes of the fault-valve pump in Lecture 12. slide 14 lecture recording, 13 Oct

The alteration is quartz, sericite, iron carbonates (ankerite, siderite), arsenopyrite and pyrite, and sometimes a green chromium mica, . If you find fuchsite in an altered zone, there is a good chance of gold. Vanadium micas are also found in the Kalgoorlie fields. Deposits are zoned vertically: Au-Sb higher in the crust and Au-As deeper. They are in large, deep, steep faults and shear zones. So they extend a long way vertically, but not far sideways. They form long, vertical, flat orebodies, which is why some Kalgoorlie mines go deeper than 2 km. The host rocks vary from slate and schist to amphibolite, dolerite, banded iron formation and granite. Orogenic gold can also overprint earlier ores, such as Ni-Cu, VMS or SEDEX. slides 14 and 15 lecture recording, 13 Oct

At the lab test
  • The mesothermal gold suites in the lab include Kalgoorlie and Lancefield (Western Australia). Expect quartz-carbonate veins with pyrite and arsenopyrite in rock altered to sericite and carbonate.
  • Bright green fuchsite (chrome mica) in carbonate-altered rock is a strong hint of an orogenic gold system.
  • Gold may be free in quartz or locked in pyrite and arsenopyrite; Kalgoorlie ores carry gold tellurides.
slides 11, 14 and 15 lecture recording, 13 Oct lab ore suites
Memorise
  • Structurally controlled lodes; compression to transpression; 200 to 700 °C, 2 to 20 km; dilute H₂O-CO₂ ± CH₄, N₂ fluid.
  • Gold late: retrograde amphibolite to greenschist, up to 100 Myr after orogeny.
  • Alteration: quartz, sericite, ankerite or siderite, arsenopyrite, pyrite, ± fuchsite.
  • Au-Sb shallow, Au-As deep; great vertical, small lateral extent.

The big picture 26 to 39 m

Where and when

The most famous historic orogenic goldfields were the Mother Lode in California, central Victoria (Bendigo, Ballarat, Castlemaine) and Bathurst-Hill End-Sofala in NSW. Their deposits are in lines along structures: the Mother Lode follows the Sierra Nevada in one long belt. slide 5 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Precambrian provinces, slide 12
Phanerozoic provinces, slide 13

Through time, the large Precambrian provinces are the Superior Province of Canada, the Yilgarn Craton and the West African Craton. The Yilgarn alone has produced more than 50 million ounces. In the Phanerozoic, the largest are the Lachlan Fold Belt (mainly the Victorian goldfields), Central Asia (Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan), the East North China Craton, the Sierra Nevada and the Russian Far East. Smaller belts that are still profitable include the New England Fold Belt and Hodgkinson in Queensland. slides 12 and 13 (Goldfarb et al., 2001) slide notes

Case study 39 to 52 m

Hill End and Holtermann’s nugget

The orogenic goldfields of central NSW are around Wattle Flat, Sofala, Hill End and Stuart Town, and Ophir, where payable gold was first found in NSW (people still disagree about who should get the credit). Hill End is still mined today. It is a small underground mine that has operated for more than a century. Sofala is a ghost town where you can still pan for gold. slide 7 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Holtermann's "nugget" contained 3000 oz of gold. Why do geologists say it was not really a nugget?

Show what I see
  • A slab about as tall as a man, irregular and blocky, not rounded or worn by water.

It is not a nugget (a lump of gold transported in a placer). It is a piece of quartz vein full of pure gold, found on 19 October 1872 at Hawkins Hill, Hill End. At 3000 oz (93.31 kg), it is the largest single mass of gold ever recorded. It would be worth at least US$15 million today. Holtermann became very rich, and his money paid for a photographic record of the NSW goldfields that still exists. slide 9 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Hawkins Hill, early 1880s, slide 10
Hawkins Hill today, slide 10

You will need some definitions, because people often use these words carelessly. A prospect is mineralised rock that has not yet been shown to be profitable. A deposit has been mined, or has a proven profitable resource that has not yet been mined. Workings are excavations made only to find or extract ore. A mine is a place where ore was or is being extracted. A field is a group of deposits or mines with a similar geological setting. slide 8

Case study 52 to 65 m

The Yilgarn Craton

The Yilgarn is an Archean area of granites and greenstones, made of three superterranes. From west to east they are the West Yilgarn, Southern Cross and the Eastern Goldfields. All are 3.0 to 2.6 Ga. Most of the gold is in the Eastern Goldfields, in greenstones of 2.71 to 2.67 Ga. The gold is hosted in greenstones, granites, granitic gneiss and banded iron formation. It is in orogenic, intrusion-related and porphyry systems that formed over a wide range of temperature and pressure. Boddington, in the west, is the large Au-Cu exception. So far, it is a deposit with no neighbours. slides 16 to 19 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Two things make exploration in the Yilgarn difficult. First, a very long history of weathering has left deep regolith, in some places weathered to 200 m, so you must drill through it to reach fresh rock. Second, the gold is not on the large crustal faults themselves. It is on the smaller structures that branch off them, often where structures cross. Structure and timing matter more than host rock: at Kalgoorlie the host is mafic volcanic rock, but in other places it is BIF or granite, as at Granny Smith. slides 16 to 19 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Case study 65 to 78 m

The Victorian goldfields

Victoria's gold is in the Palaeozoic Lachlan Orogen, mostly in turbidites in greenschist facies. These are repetitive sandstone, siltstone and shale, with little visible alteration. The province is divided into nine zones bounded by major faults (Moyston, Avoca, Heathcote, Wellington). The largest deposits are in the Stawell to Ballarat zones. As in the Yilgarn, the deposits are on structures that branch off the large faults. At Walhalla (Woods Point), the gold crystallised along the contacts of a dolerite dyke. The dyke arrived earlier and acted as an aquitard, stopping the rising fluid. slide 21 slide notes lecture recording, 13 Oct

The numbers are very large. Ballarat produced about 200 000 kg (6.43 Moz; the slide values it at about US$33 billion today). Bendigo produced about 700 000 kg (22.5 Moz; about US$120 billion on the slide, or about US$90 billion at US$4000 an ounce). Much of it was alluvial gold, mined before the deep reefs. The gold ages range from late Ordovician to Devonian, and are different in each zone, because each zone was deformed at a different time. Exploration today focuses on the west, around Stawell, where the gold-bearing rocks continue under younger cover. slides 22 and 24 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Case study 78 to 91 m

Stawell: gold around a basalt dome

Stawell, on the western edge of the Victorian goldfields, started in the 1850s. Its largest mine, Magdala (about 4 Moz), has produced gold from more than 1600 m below the surface. The gold is around the edges of a large dome of Ordovician basalt. Basalt is rigid and hardly deforms, while the sediments around it fold and shear. So spaces open where the difference in rock strength is greatest, and quartz veins and gold fill them. The continuation of the Stawell corridor to the south, under cover, is an important target. slide 25 slide notes

Field task · two generations of quartzslide 25

There are at least two sets of quartz here. Which came first? Which one probably contains the gold?

Show what I see
  • Many thin, irregular, deformed white quartz veins in black carbonaceous shale.
  • A thicker quartz vein that cuts straight through them.

The thin, deformed veins are earlier, and the thick vein that cuts them is later. This is a cross-cutting relationship. This is the mineralised vein of the Hanging Wall lode, cutting earlier barren quartz. In orogenic gold, the vein with gold is usually a late one, so it is important to know which generation you are looking at. slide 25 lecture recording, 13 Oct

The giants 91 to 104 m

Muruntau and the largest gold deposits

Muruntau, in the desert of Uzbekistan, is the world's largest open-cut gold mine (about 3.5 × 2.5 km and 560 m deep) and the largest orogenic gold deposit, with about 150 million ounces in total. The government owns 100% of it. Local people had mined turquoise there on a small scale for thousands of years. In 1958, a soil geochemical survey found arsenic and gold anomalies, and mining started in 1967. The host rock is Ordovician to early Silurian marine clastic rocks, siltstone and mudstone, similar to Victoria. The alteration is fine-grained and mostly argillic. It produces more than 2 million ounces per year. slides 26 and 27 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Concept 2 104 to 117 m

Where the fluid and the gold come from

For a long time, people thought that the fluid for orogenic gold was metamorphic water, released when rocks are heated and lose water (this is how Lecture 12 explained it). This lecture gives the other view. Many experimental and isotope studies in the last 20 years show that granites are the source of the fluid that carries the gold. The sulphur isotopes look magmatic, and the trace elements that travel with the gold (Bi, Te, As, Mo, Sb, Sn, W) are typical granite elements. The granites are usually buried, not exposed. slide 29 lecture recording, 13 Oct

The gold itself seems to come from the country rock. Most geologists now agree that it starts as tiny inclusions in pyrite, in carbonaceous mudstones and shales that cover large areas. Metamorphism releases it, and convection moves it. One study in northern Finland took samples every 50 m, for 10 km on each side of a deposit. Gold increased steadily towards the deposit. This shows that the gold was collected from the rock around it. slide 29 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Faults and shear zones focus the fluid. They also provide impermeable or reactive barriers that make gold be deposited. Dolerite dykes and sills are common traps. Fluid moves by seismic pumping (Richard Sibson's model, developed further by Stephen Cox). A whole deposit may form in only 10 000 to 100 000 years. slide 30

Variation 117 to 130 m

Carlin-type gold

This lecture treats deposits as a type of orogenic gold. In Nevada, the gold is in sub-greenschist argillites and limestones, with strong silicification and less kaolinisation. It formed 2 to 3 km deep at 200 to 300 °C, from dilute fluid, during Cenozoic back-arc extension (Basin and Range). The deposits are in lines called trends: Carlin, Getchell, Battle Mountain-Eureka, Independence and Alligator Ridge. Southern China also has them, but none have been found in Australia. Mining them built two of the largest gold companies, Barrick and Newmont. slides 31 and 32 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Field task · Carlin oreslide 33

This is gold ore. Find the gold.

Show what I see
  • A dark, fine-grained carbonaceous sedimentary rock with a little fine pyrite. No visible gold at all.

You cannot, and that is the point. Carlin gold is so fine that it is almost invisible. It is locked in pyrite that contains arsenic. What you can see are the arsenic and antimony minerals that occur with it. The ore looks like waste, so Carlin mines must assay all the time to know that they are still in ore. slides 31 and 33 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Case study 130 to 144 m

Tuena: reading generations of veins

Tuena, between Bathurst and Goulburn in NSW, is a historic goldfield (about 100 000 oz). It was studied in an honours project in 2020. It is along the Godolphin-Copperhannia fault corridor, with many small deposits along smaller faults. An unknown adit (tunnel), more than 100 m long and not on any geological record, gave a rare continuous 3D view through the geology. It was mapped and logged in detail. slides 36 to 38 lecture recording, 13 Oct

Field task · veins in the aditslide 39

These pale quartz veins are parallel to the foliation and are folded. Did they form before or after the main deformation?

Show what I see
  • Pale quartz in pods and lenses, folded and pulled into the foliation. Some have lost their connection to the rest of the vein (rootless).

These veins formed before or during deformation: early, refolded veins and syn-kinematic veins. They are not the gold veins. At Tuena, as across the Lachlan, the gold is in late, post-kinematic, flat, carbonate-rich veins that cut the foliation. slides 39 and 40 lecture recording, 13 Oct

What are the bright yellow grains, and where are they?

Show what I see
  • Bright yellow, high-reflectance grains, irregular and long.
  • In lines along thin fractures, plus a few separate specks.

This is gold in very small veinlets and disseminated grains, filling late fractures. The gold came in after the rock was already deformed and fractured, as the vein history shows. slide 40
