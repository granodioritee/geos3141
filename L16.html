Silver veins and basin brines

This lecture covers four groups of base-metal deposits in sedimentary rocks. CMPV deposits are silver-rich veins in deformed metasediments, at the far end of a granite system. MVT, Irish-type and Kupferschiefer deposits are not related to igneous rocks at all. In these deposits, warm basin brines carry zinc, lead or copper until they meet something that reduces them. Start with an MVT, because its process is easy to see.

Four families

The four styles are polymetallic veins hosted in clastic metasediments (), Mississippi Valley-type (), the related Irish-type deposits, and Kupferschiefer-type copper hosted in sediment. CMPV ore is in veins in metasandstone, metasiltstone, metaconglomerate and even metavolcanic rocks. MVT and Irish ore replaces carbonate. Kupferschiefer ore is very fine grained and finely banded in carbonaceous black shale. slides 1 and 2 lecture recording, 27 Oct

Concept 1 13 to 26 m

CMPV deposits

CMPV deposits are often called polymetallic veins related to granite intrusions. They are close to felsic intrusions, usually large batholiths, and often formed from them. But they are not linked to porphyry copper systems. They are complex polymetallic deposits, with veins rich in silver and base-metal sulphides. The granite can be several kilometres away. You might drill a deposit and need to go many kilometres further to reach the granite. slide 3 lecture recording, 27 Oct

They form under mesothermal conditions, at about 6 km and 250 to 300 °C, similar to orogenic gold. The fluids have 0 to 26 wt% salinity and can be anywhere from 0 to 10 km deep. The veins are strongly controlled by structures. They are linked to fault zones, or to smaller structures that branch off very large shear zones. There are three kinds of host basin: (1) continental margins that started as embayments at the edge of a craton, on passive margins; (2) collisional boundaries at subduction zones inside continents or oceans (the Harz Mountains, Příbram); or (3) basins at the edges of continents, oceans or back arcs (Keno Hill). They are in Pb-Zn provinces that often also contain SEDEX deposits, and they form late, after compression or extension. slides 5 and 6

Webbs, Conrad and the silver giants

The classic districts are Kokanee Range, Keno Hill, Coeur d'Alene, the Harz Mountains and Freiberg in Germany, Příbram in the Czech Republic, Guangzhou in China, and the New England Orogen of NSW and Queensland. It is not a major deposit type, but these deposits are extremely rich in silver. slides 7 and 8 lecture recording, 27 Oct

Tonnages and grades from slides 8 and 9 (Coeur d'Alène is production up to 1965, more than 100 Mt). Contained silver = Mt × g/t.

In NSW, both main CMPV deposits are in the southern New England Orogen. Conrad (458 000 t at 600 g/t Ag, 8% Pb, 4% Zn, 1.5% Cu and 1.5% Sn), near Inverell, is hosted in the granite itself. Webbs (1.49 Mt at 245 g/t Ag, 0.71% Pb, 1.56% Zn and 0.27% Cu), near Emmaville, is in dark metasiltstone away from the granite. Deeper in Webbs, drilling started to find wolframite and cassiterite. So the vein system changes towards the granite-related Sn-W system of Lecture 15. Silver, lead and zinc are the low-temperature end, far from the granite. Tin and tungsten are the high-temperature end, close to it. slide 9 lecture recording, 27 Oct

In the hand 39 to 52 m

CMPV host rocks, alteration and minerals

The host rocks are thick, repetitive sequences of fine- to medium-grained clastic sediments. They have minor carbonate, mafic and tuff units, and are metamorphosed to at least greenschist facies (at Webbs, dark grey to black carbonaceous rock). Wall-rock alteration (sericite, silica, pyrite) is only a few metres wide around the veins, so it does not help much to find the ore. Large zones of hydrothermal bleaching, caused by loss of iron, are much better. They can extend for kilometres. slide 10 lecture recording, 27 Oct

Field task · a CMPV coreslide 13

Look at A. Describe the colour of the host rock and what crosses it. What would you record in your log?

Show what I see
  • Pale, bleached siltstone (the Fe has been removed).
  • A quartz vein with siderite and disseminated sulphide, a thin massive sulphide veinlet, and dark green chlorite.

You would record a bleached siltstone cut by a quartz-siderite vein with disseminated sulphide and a massive sulphide veinlet, plus chlorite. This is typical CMPV gangue and ore, and the bleaching helps you find the ore over a larger area. slide 13 lecture recording, 27 Oct

The ore is mostly galena, sphalerite and tetrahedrite-tennantite. There are minor amounts of pyrite, chalcopyrite, arsenopyrite, stibnite, native silver, and a complex group of silver sulphosalts (pyrargyrite, stephanite, bournonite), especially around Freiberg and Příbram. , the silver-rich tetrahedrite named after Freiberg, usually forms late: silver comes after the main sulphides, at a lower temperature. Near granites, there is also tin: sphalerite with exsolved stannite. Arsenopyrite forms early, during alteration, but it causes problems, because smelters charge penalties for arsenic. The gangue is quartz and carbonate (mostly siderite, with minor dolomite, ankerite and calcite), plus minor barite, fluorite and magnetite. Calcite and dolomite usually form late, when meteoric water floods the hydrothermal system as it stops. slides 11 to 13 slide notes lecture recording, 27 Oct

Mechanism 52 to 65 m

CMPV textures and how they form

CMPV veins have several phases, with complex sequences and different textures: , , colloform banding and textures. The sulphides occur as coarse massive sulphide and as fine disseminated grains. Later deformation, brecciation or shearing can destroy the primary textures. slide 14

There are three models for how they form. The real answer is probably a mix, because most studies look at only one deposit. (1) Magmatic differentiation: a volatile fluid from a magma that is fractionating (often mafic) contains a lot of chalcophile Ag, Pb, Zn and Cu. It flows up faults. Mafic and lamprophyre dykes are evidence for this. (2) Deep crustal faults: deep faults are the main pathways for carbon, lead and fluid from deep sources (shown by isotopes). The ore forms by local boiling and mixing with cooler meteoric water and water from the upper crust, in smaller structures, as in orogenic gold. (3) Related to intrusions: a nearby crystallising intrusion only provides the heat, at 250 to 300 °C. The metals and fluids are meteoric and metamorphic waters from the country rock. This last model is used for Webbs. slides 15 to 18 slide notes lecture recording, 27 Oct

Concept 2 65 to 78 m

Mississippi Valley-type

MVT deposits are named after classic districts in the Mississippi River area of the central USA. They contain about 24% of the world's Pb-Zn resources hosted in sediment and volcanic rocks. They are epigenetic: they formed tens to hundreds of millions of years after their host rocks. They form at low temperature (about 50 to 250 °C, mostly 90 to 150 °C) from basin brines with 10 to 30 wt% NaCl. They have no link in space or time to igneous rocks. This makes them different from skarns and other Pb-Zn deposits related to intrusions. They are usually stratabound, and in some places stratiform. slides 19 and 20 slide notes

Deposits range from 0.2 Mt to 95.3 Mt (Navan), with an average of about 7 Mt at 7.9% Pb + Zn. They occur in districts with up to 400 deposits: if you find one, you will find more. Silver is a minor by-product (median 37 g/t). Some are mined for other minerals: fluorite in Illinois, barite in England. In Australia, MVTs contain about 8% of Zn-Pb-Ag resources (about 10.7 Mt Zn, 5.9 Mt Pb and 4888 t Ag). The Lennard Shelf in WA has deposits of 2 to 20 Mt, usually 2 to 4 Mt at about 12% Pb + Zn. Sorby Hills is the largest MVT resource in Australia. The recording says that a shallow aquifer makes it difficult to mine. slides 21 and 22 lecture recording, 27 Oct

Most MVTs are in platform carbonates on passive margins, in the forelands of orogens, in contraction or thrust belts. The Canning Basin (Lennard Shelf) is an exception. Its deposits formed during extensional faulting, as the basin changed into a passive margin. slide 23 lecture recording, 27 Oct

In the hand 78 to 91 m

MVT ores and how they form

The host rocks are dolostone or limestone in platform carbonate sequences. Deposits in dolostone are usually larger and richer in Pb, Zn and Ag, probably because fluid flows through dolostone more easily (Navan, in limestone, is the exception). The alteration is dolomitisation, dissolution of the host and brecciation. Silicification is minor, and is more common in hotter systems. Early acidic fluid dissolves the carbonate and makes collapse breccias and holes. So most of the permeability is secondary, and the ore fills it. slide 25 slide notes lecture recording, 27 Oct

The ore is simple: sphalerite and galena with less pyrite and marcasite, some silver and little copper. The Viburnum Trend in Missouri is the complex exception, with Cu, Co, Ni and Sb sulphides. The gangue is carbonate (dolomite, siderite, ankerite, calcite), with little or no barite and fluorite, although some deposits are mostly barite or fluorite. Sulphide mostly replaces carbonate, and some fills open space. The grain size ranges from extremely fine to crystals more than a metre long. Caves can contain calcite crystals 2 m across (test for gas first, because hydrocarbons can leave carbon monoxide). slides 26 and 27 lecture recording, 27 Oct

Field task · a brecciaslide 28

The blocks are carbonate, and the dark seams are sulphide. What made the breccia, and when did the sulphide arrive?

Show what I see
  • Angular blocks of pale carbonate that fit back together, with thin dark sulphide seams between them.

This is a dissolution collapse breccia. The carbonate was dissolved, and the roof collapsed into blocks. Then hydrothermal fluid altered the breccia and filled and replaced the gaps with sulphide. The collapse came first, and the ore came second. Upper Silesia, Poland. slide 28 (Leach et al., 2005)

How do they form? Several basin brines mix. Some contain reduced sulphur and some contain metals. The metals, and the sulphur, come from the clastic rocks of the basin. The carbonate host was once a reduced environment with a lot of hydrocarbons and organic matter. Oxidised basin water carried metals as chloride or sulphate complexes. When it met the organic matter, the redox reaction made the complexes unstable, and Pb and Zn sulphides were deposited. Ore formation is linked to major tectonic events in the crust, when a stable carbonate platform is suddenly uplifted or squeezed. Variation 91 to 104 m

Irish-type

Irish-type deposits are between two types. They are stratabound Pb-Zn deposits hosted in carbonate and controlled by structures, with features of both SEDEX and MVT. They are often treated as a type of MVT. They were recognised as a separate type in the 1970s and early 1980s, when the large deposits of the Irish Midlands were interpreted as forming during or soon after sedimentation. Unlike MVTs, they are closely linked to volcanism in space and time, and most mineralisation happens during diagenesis, in sediment that is partly or completely hardened. slides 31 and 32 lecture recording, 27 Oct

Deposits are usually less than 10 Mt at 5 to 6% Zn, 1 to 2% Pb and 30 g/t Ag (up to 90 g/t), with traces of Cu, Cd, As, Ni and Co. The slide notes give Navan as 90 Mt at 10.5% Pb + Zn, Lisheen as 14.4 Mt at 11.7% Zn and 2% Pb, and Galmoy as 6.7 Mt at 11.9% Zn + Pb. They form on carbonate ramps in extensional basins on continental margins that are being stretched. They are concentrated on the blocks that dropped down along normal faults, some in relay systems between faults. These faults were the pathways for the fluid. The host rocks are clean carbonates (mainly limestone, with little clay) in mixed sequences of carbonate and siliciclastic rocks. There is dolomitisation, Mn halos in some deposits, and rare silicification. slides 32 to 34 slide notes

Most of the sulphur comes from bacteria. The ore is sphalerite and galena with minor pyrite, chalcopyrite, arsenopyrite, tennantite and Ni-Co minerals (cobaltite, niccolite). Silver is in tennantite, galena and sphalerite. Where the ore is exposed at the surface, the oxide zone adds cerussite, anglesite, smithsonite, limonite and pyromorphite, and this zone paid for early mining. The gangue is dolomite, calcite and quartz. Barite is common, and in some places it is the main product. Fluorite is extremely rare. There are two models for how they formed. One is syngenetic deposition on the seafloor (stratiform shape, bedded and broken sulphides, similar ages for ore and host). The other is diagenetic to epigenetic replacement (replacement and open-space textures, few laminated sulphides, alteration above the lenses, no oxidation on the seafloor). The real answer is probably both. slides 35 to 37 lecture recording, 27 Oct

Variation 104 to 117 m

Kupferschiefer: copper hosted in sediment

deposits, also called stratiform sediment-hosted Cu, are hosted in reduced, carbonaceous sedimentary rocks from shallow sea or lake settings. These rocks are full of organic matter that formed in place. The ore zones are thin, usually less than 30 m and often less than 3 m, and they follow the layering. They are the second most important source of copper after porphyries, about 23% of world production. They are also important for silver and cobalt, and some contain Pb, Zn, U, Au and PGE. Grades average about 2% Cu and 50 ppm Ag, with by-products of Pb, Zn, Au, Ni, Pt-Pd, Re and Se. Poland's Kupferschiefer and the Central African Copperbelt are the largest. slides 38 and 39 slide notes lecture recording, 27 Oct

They form in rifts inside continents, at the boundary between oxidised red-bed sandstones deposited by wind and more reduced shales, carbonates and evaporites. The rocks were deposited within 20 to 30° of the old equator, in desert conditions. The host rocks are siltstone, shale, wind-blown sandstone and dolomite above hematite-rich red beds. Bleaching extends for kilometres. You can follow it into red beds of the same age. This shows that the sandstones were once red and lost their Fe³⁺. Fast rifting and subsidence made basin fluids move. Copper was deposited at the redox boundary between oxidised and reduced rocks, helped by bacteria that reduced sulphate in an environment with sulphide (euxinic). slides 40 to 42, 45 lecture recording, 27 Oct

The ore is chalcocite and covellite, with bornite, chalcopyrite, native copper, galena, sphalerite with little Fe, and silver minerals. Oxide ore near the surface is malachite, azurite, chrysocolla and cuprite. The gangue is dolomite, calcite, anhydrite, gypsum, barite and marcasite. The ore is fine-grained and disseminated. It cements grains, and occurs in veinlets and replacements, and sometimes as nodules where it replaced anhydrite. Zones without mineralisation contain framboidal pyrite. slides 43 and 44

Case study 117 to 130 m

Manuka

Manuka, about 90 km SSW of Cobar in NSW, is a silver deposit with three open cuts (Manuka, Boundary and Boundary South). It is on the western Winduck Shelf of the Cobar Basin, a basin inside the craton, in the Palaeozoic Lachlan Orogen. It is a low-grade Pb-Zn deposit hosted in carbonate, similar to MVT and Irish types. It formed on a stable shelf with open platform carbonate and reef, and it is not linked to igneous rocks: the limestone was deposited on top of much older, eroded granite. slides 46 to 50 lecture recording, 27 Oct

It has two styles. Secondary oxide ore is in clays with Fe and Mn above the limestone. These clays are what was left when the impure limestone weathered and dissolved, and they kept its silver (about 30 g/t). This is the ore that is mined. Primary sulphide ore occurs in two forms. One is mineralisation rich in pyrite, in black calcareous shale just above the carbonate. The other is galena-sphalerite in fossil-rich dolomitic limestone at the top of the carbonate sequence (these are more like an Irish type and an MVT, respectively). The ore is sphalerite with little Fe, pyrite and galena, with silver in acanthite (Ag₂S). The gangue is dolomite, calcite, quartz, smoky quartz and minor barite. slides 48 to 51 lecture recording, 27 Oct

Classify the deposit0 / 6

Memorise
  • CMPV: Ag-Pb-Zn veins in greenschist metasediments, distal to batholiths, 250 to 300 °C; bleaching is the vector. Webbs, Conrad, Keno Hill, Coeur d’Alene.
  • MVT: epigenetic Zn-Pb in platform carbonates, 90 to 150 °C, 10 to 30 wt% NaCl brines, no igneous link; organic matter traps the ore.
  • Irish: diagenetic, normal-fault controlled, barite common, fluorite rare.
  • Kupferschiefer: thin Cu ore at the red bed / reduced shale redox front; ~23% of world Cu.
