How a small amount of sulphide becomes rich ore

Lecture 7 showed how nickel goes into a sulphide liquid. This lecture asks how rich that liquid can become. It then looks at the most famous places where magmatic ore is found: a meteorite crater in Canada, flood basalts in Siberia, a layered intrusion 400 km across in South Africa, chromite pods in old oceanic mantle near Tumut, and the weathered tropical soils that now supply most of the world's nickel. We start with the number that controls the grade.

The R-factor: how rich the sulphide becomes

The measures how much silicate magma has reacted (equilibrated) with a given amount of sulphide melt. A large R-factor means a small amount of sulphide melt has met a lot of magma. So that small amount of sulphide has collected a high concentration of nickel, copper and PGE. A small R-factor means there is a lot of sulphide melt, and each part of it contains only a small concentration. slide 1

So for magmatic nickel sulphide deposits, you want a large R-factor: all the available nickel and copper goes into a small volume of sulphide. For nickel laterites you want the opposite, a small R-factor. Then the nickel stays in the silicate (in olivine), and weathering concentrates it later. slide 2 lecture recording, 29 Sep

The starting magma is also important. Basalts contain about 100 to 500 ppm Ni, and ultramafic magmas about 2000 to 4000 ppm. Try the mixer at the top with both. slide 19

Concept 1 12 to 26 m

Deposits hosted in mafic intrusions

Sudbury, Norilsk, Voisey's Bay and Jinchuan are hosted in intrusions. The ore occurs in three places: in lens-shaped irregular areas at the lower contact between gabbro and wall rock (the contaminated zones), in layers within layered gabbro, and in irregular branches and groups of veins that go into the rocks around the intrusion. The ore is zoned. At the bottom there is massive and brecciated sulphide (more than 70% sulphide). Above that is network sulphide (40 to 70%), and then disseminated sulphide (15 to 40%). The sulphide is about 70% pyrrhotite, 15% pentlandite and 15% chalcopyrite. slide 3

In massive ore, coarse pentlandite forms patches and veins in pyrrhotite. This fits with the ore cooling from one Fe-Ni-S liquid that was originally uniform. Disseminated ore is droplets of that liquid that were trapped. Metamorphism has hardly changed these deposits, but in some places hydrothermal fluids have moved metals, especially PGE along faults. Where a granite rich in arsenic intrudes, platinum combines with the arsenic and forms secondary zones of platinum arsenide (sperrylite). slide 3 lecture recording, 29 Sep

These systems get their magma from the mantle through deep structures in thick crust. Magma rises through deep, middle and shallow chambers, sills and dykes. On the way, crustal rocks contaminate it with silica, sulphur and Fe³⁺, and also carbon from coal or sulphate from evaporites. This makes the magma saturated in sulphide early. Gabbroic types have high Cu/Ni (Sudbury, Norilsk), and ultrabasic types have low Cu/Ni. When the sulphide liquid itself fractionates, Cu/Ni becomes higher further from the main ore. slide 4 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Case study 26 to 40 m

Sudbury: ore from a meteorite impact

Sudbury, in Canada, is an unusual mafic body in a deformed basin shape. The best explanation is a meteorite impact. The meteorite hit an area where the crust was already being pulled apart. So the fractures went all the way down to the mantle, and mantle melt rose through them. The result is the Sudbury Igneous Complex (SIC). It has norite at the base, with sulphides disseminated through it and in massive lenses. There is a contaminated sublayer at its edge, granophyre (a fine-grained granitic rock) on top, and the Onaping tuffs above it all. The Onaping tuffs are a breccia of impact material that fell back into the crater. There is not one deposit but many, all around the rim. slides 5 and 6 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Field task · a hand specimen from Sudburyslide 7

The lines spread out across the surface like a fan. What kind of structure is this? What does it tell you about Sudbury?

Show the reasoning

This looks like a shatter cone: cone-shaped fracture surfaces with lines on them, made by the shock wave of a very fast impact. The excellent shatter cones around Sudbury are part of the evidence that the basin is an impact structure. Normal igneous or tectonic processes do not make them.

Case studies 40 to 54 m

Norilsk, Jinchuan and Monchegorsk

Norilsk-Talnakh, in northern Siberia, is part of the Permian-Triassic Siberian flood basalts. Very large lithospheric faults let magma rise into sills, such as the Talnakh intrusion, which has the largest deposits. The lavas are above them. The ore field is about 100 km by 50 km, and there are more deposits outside it. Mining and exploration there will continue for many generations. Massive sulphide is at the base of the intrusions and goes down into the footwall. Disseminated sulphide is in taxitic gabbro-dolerite, which has an irregular, mixed texture. The key factor is contamination. The sills intruded evaporites, and the sulphate in them supplied sulphur. The country rocks also supplied silica and Fe³⁺. Because the whole system is preserved, it can be mined to great depth. slides 11 to 13 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Jinchuan is on the southern edge of the Sino-Korean craton, in a Proterozoic rift where an ocean was starting to open. It is not one intrusion but a series of pipe-shaped ultramafic bodies, with ore at their bases. It supplies most of China's nickel. slide 14 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Concept 2 54 to 68 m

How the deposits form, and the economics

Ni sulphide deposits are magmatic: they crystallise directly from a silicate melt, with no hydrothermal stage. The silicate melt becomes saturated in sulphur, and sulphide droplets that do not mix with it form, in three situations: when its temperature drops, when silica increases (contamination by silica-rich crust), or when sulphur increases (contamination by crustal sulphur). The amount of Ni, Cu and PGE in the droplets depends on three things: the metal content of the magma at the start, the partition coefficient between the two melts, and the ratio of sulphide to silicate and how well they mix (the R-factor). The droplets collect at the base of the conduit or chamber, because sulphide liquid (SG about 4.4) is much denser than silicate liquid (about 3.3). Flow separation also helps. Then the sulphide liquid crystallises, and it can separate into Cu-rich and Ni-rich parts. slides 18 and 19 lecture recording, 29 Sep

The minerals control the economics. Pentlandite that separates (exsolves) late from pyrrhotite helps processing, because it forms coarser pentlandite. Metamorphism can partly replace iron sulphides with magnetite in matrix ores. Deep oxidation replaces pyrrhotite and pentlandite with (Ni₂FeS₄). Fluids with As and CO₂ enter along faults and shear zones. In the mine, complex shapes, especially in extrusive deposits, make it expensive to define and extract the ore. Pyrrhotite must be managed, because it oxidises so quickly in waste piles. slide 17 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Memorise
  • Intrusion-hosted zoning: massive and brecciated (over 70% sulphide), network (40 to 70%), disseminated (15 to 40%).
  • Sulphide is about 70% pyrrhotite, 15% pentlandite, 15% chalcopyrite.
  • Sulphide liquid SG about 4.4 vs silicate liquid about 3.3.
  • Starting Ni: basalt 100 to 500 ppm; ultramafic 2000 to 4000 ppm.

Concept 3 68 to 82 m

Platinum group elements

The six PGEs form two groups that behave differently. The PPGE (platinum, palladium, rhodium) go strongly into sulphide. The IPGE (iridium, osmium, ruthenium) usually stay in the host rock. Platinum is not only used for jewellery: much of it is used in catalysts and engines. South Africa produces about five times more platinum than any other country. This is why the world kept buying from South Africa, even during apartheid. Russia produces the most palladium, almost all from Norilsk. slides 20 to 22 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Primary PGE deposits have several styles. (1) Orthomagmatic deposits, formed by magma mixing in layered intrusions: the Bushveld's UG2, Merensky Reef and Platreef; the J-M Reef of Stillwater; and the Great Dyke of Zimbabwe. (2) Deposits related to contamination, where PGE are by-products: Norilsk, Sudbury, Kambalda and Thompson. (3) A rare deuteric style, the hortonolite pipes of the Bushveld (Onverwacht, Mooihoek, Driekop). These are pipes of iron-rich olivine, and they were the first hard-rock platinum deposits ever mined, in the early 1900s. slide 23 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Case study 82 to 96 m

The Bushveld Complex

The Bushveld, north of Johannesburg, is a very large layered intrusion about 400 km across. It has western, far western, eastern and northern limbs. Its Merensky Reef and UG2 chromitite are the world's main source of platinum and rhodium. It also has world-class deposits of magnetite, chromite, vanadium, titanium and phosphorus, and its later granites supply South Africa's tin. Heavy elements, sulphide droplets and oxide minerals settled out while it was cooling early. It intruded the Transvaal Supergroup, which is rich in carbonate and was a source of contamination. slides 25 and 26 lecture recording, 29 Sep

The intrusion is layered. From the bottom: a lower zone; the critical zone (with the LG6 and UG2 chromitites, and the Merensky Reef near its top); the main zone, which is about 2 km of almost the same gabbronorite (in drill core it is the same rock for 2 km); then the upper zone; and a roof of granophyre and felsite. The reefs are only a few metres thick, but they continue for more than 200 km. Each new batch of magma deposits dense chromite at the base, and plagioclase floats to form anorthosite above it. This makes the repeated (rhythmic) layering. A hot new batch of magma can melt and erode the layer below. This makes dips called potholes in the Merensky Reef. slides 27 and 28 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Show the reasoning

The black layers are chromitite and the white layers are anorthosite. Each new batch of magma crystallised chromite, which sank, and plagioclase, which floated. Concept 4 96 to 108 m

Chromium, vanadium and titanium

Chromium is in chromite, which has a complex chemistry. Vanadium and titanium are in magnetite and ilmenite. Most economic V and Ti deposits are separate layers rich in oxides, up to several metres thick, in large layered mafic-ultramafic intrusions. Titanium also comes from mineral sands, and vanadium from some sedimentary uranium deposits. The oxide layers need an oxidising magma, which is the opposite of the reduced conditions needed for sulphides. Metallurgical-grade chromium comes from podiform chromitites in the upper mantle part of ophiolites. The Bushveld's layered chromitites give chemical-grade chromium. slide 29 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Many conditions are needed to make an economic oxide deposit. You need: a high starting concentration of Cr, Ti and V (or a constant new supply); early crystallisation of chromite, magnetite and ilmenite; a way to concentrate them (gravity settling, segregation, or crystals rolling down a sloping chamber floor); stable temperature, pressure and oxidation state for long enough to build thick layers; a way to remove the lighter minerals, such as floating plagioclase; and preservation. slide 40 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Case study 108 to 122 m

Podiform chromitites

Podiform chromitites are in the upper mantle, usually near the boundary between crust and mantle in oceanic lithosphere. This is why they are only found in ophiolites. Like everything else in this lecture, they form in conduits. A basaltic magma flows through a channel in harzburgite and reacts with it, which leaves a rim of dunite around the channel. Chromite crystallises as magma batches mix again and again. It is denser than the magma, so it collects as a pod. Nodular ore is typical. Turkey, Kazakhstan, India and Albania mine podiform chromite. slide 36 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Concept 5 122 to 132 m

Phosphorus: Khibiny

Primary phosphorus deposits occur high in the sequence of large layered intrusions. They also occur in large, very alkaline, reduced syenite intrusions. The ore mineral is apatite, in typical lenses of magmatic origin. The chemistry around it is unusual: Na-rich pyroxenes and amphiboles, Ti-Zr silicates, REE carbonates, Nb oxides, even K sulphides, and very soluble fluorides such as red villiaumite (NaF). Rare earth elements are important by-products. slide 41 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Concept 6 132 to 144 m

Nickel laterites

Laterites now provide most of the world's nickel production and about 70% of nickel resources. They are in Indonesia, the Philippines, New Caledonia, Cuba and Australia, with potential in PNG and across Southeast Asia. The source rocks are ultramafic, mostly serpentinised olivine-rich rock in ophiolites or komatiites. They contain about 0.2 to 0.4% Ni, which replaces Mg in olivine. Strong tropical weathering, now or in the past, removes the magnesium and silica and concentrates the nickel. Laterites were known in the 1950s, but only newer processing methods made them profitable. The best processing method depends on which mineral contains the Ni and Co. Garnierite ore is smelted. Clay ore uses pressure acid leaching (PAL), which uses a lot of acid. Limonitic ore uses a hydrometallurgical ammonia leach. slides 44 and 45 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Field task · Shevchenko deposit, Uralsslide 44

The face is weathered serpentinite with grades of 1.47 to 1.88% Ni. What was this rock before weathering? Why is it ore now?

Show the reasoning

It was an ultramafic rock, serpentinised peridotite or dunite, with only about 0.2 to 0.4% Ni in its olivine. Strong weathering removed the magnesium and silica and left the nickel. So the nickel became several times more concentrated. It is attached to fine goethite and in clays. No sulphide was ever involved.

Profiles can be about 100 m deep. Mature profiles form in drier climates, such as Western Australia. Immature profiles form in humid climates, such as central Africa. If the water table drops later (rejuvenation), the nickel can be enriched further. Deposits are classified by the main mineral that contains the Ni: garnierite (a talc-like Ni silicate: higher Ni, low Co), smectite clays (low to moderate Ni), or oxide (goethite: low Ni, high Co). slides 47, 48 and 50 lecture recording, 29 Sep

Memorise
  • Laterites: most world Ni production, about 70% of resources; source rock 0.2 to 0.4% Ni in olivine.
  • Saprolite has the highest Ni; the limonite zone the highest Co.
  • Garnierite (smelting), clay (PAL), limonite (ammonia leach).
